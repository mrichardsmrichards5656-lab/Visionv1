Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON)

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a rare genetic cause of sight loss.

What is Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON)?

Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON) is a rare genetic cause of sight loss. A gene change, usually in the mitochondria (the parts of a cell that produce energy), stops certain cells in the eye producing the energy they need. These cells carry signals through the optic nerve to the brain, and central vision is lost.

How it affects vision

LHON often starts with painless blurring of central vision in one eye. The second eye usually follows within months. Colour vision fades and there is a dense blur in the centre of vision, often leaving sight worse than the top letter of the eye chart. Vision usually then stays stable. Some recovery is possible in some people, typically as small islands of clearer vision, and how likely that is depends on which gene change you have.

Getting a diagnosis

An eye specialist examines the optic nerves and tests the field of vision and colour vision. Nearly one in five people have no visible change in the optic nerve at first. A genetic blood test looks for the gene changes that cause LHON; three of them account for about 90% of cases. Because the first symptom is sudden painless blurring, get any such change checked urgently.

Who it affects

LHON mainly affects young men; the typical age when sight is lost is 20 to 30, and over 90% of those affected lose vision before age 50. It is usually passed down through the mother's side of the family, although only about 60% of families know of an affected relative. Carrying the gene change does not mean you will lose sight: about 50% of men and 90% of women with it do not. Smoking and heavy drinking, especially binge drinking, are linked with a higher risk.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Idebenone (Raxone) — NICE Recommended

In August 2025 NICE recommended idebenone tablets for treating sight loss from LHON in people aged 12 and over (TA1093). It is the first LHON treatment NICE has recommended for the NHS in England, and it is prescribed through specialist centres. It helps cells produce energy, and trials show it can improve vision and slow deterioration in some people. Ask your specialist about availability in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland.

✅ NHS Available

Genetic Testing and Family Advice

Genetic testing confirms the diagnosis and which gene change is involved, which affects the outlook. A genetics service can explain what the result means for relatives on the mother's side. A positive test in a relative cannot predict whether or when they would develop symptoms.

🌅 Horizon

Gene Therapy (Lenadogene Nolparvovec)

A gene therapy injected into the eye is being tested for the most common gene change in LHON (called m.11778G>A, in the ND4 gene). As of May 2026 the company said it had not received marketing authorisation in any country, so it is not available on the NHS and remains experimental.

Practical guides

Get Sudden Central Blur Checked Urgently

Painless blurring in the centre of vision in one eye needs an urgent eye examination. Call NHS 111 or contact an optician offering urgent appointments the same day. If LHON runs in your family, say so.

Do Not Smoke and Avoid Heavy Drinking

Smoking and heavy or binge drinking are linked with a higher risk of sight loss in people who carry an LHON gene change. This advice applies to relatives who carry the change as well as to people already affected.

Ask About Idebenone

If you are 12 or over and have sight loss from LHON, ask your eye specialist whether idebenone is suitable for you and how to be referred to a centre that prescribes it.

Talk to Relatives on Your Mother's Side

Brothers, sisters and other relatives through the mother's line may carry the same gene change. Genetic counselling on the NHS can help families decide about testing.

Mention Other Symptoms

LHON is occasionally linked with heart rhythm or neurological problems. Tell your doctor about palpitations, tremor, numbness or weakness so they can arrange checks.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with Leber Hereditary Optic Neuropathy (LHON)

Driving

LHON affects central vision in both eyes, which most people need for driving. You must be able to meet the eyesight standard for driving, so do not drive until you have been told it is safe, and ask your doctor or optician about telling the DVLA. In Northern Ireland the DVA applies.

Study and Starting Work

LHON often arrives during education or early in a career. Tell your school, college or university disability service early. Students may qualify for Disabled Students' Allowance, which is assessed, and Access to Work can help with equipment and support in a job.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
