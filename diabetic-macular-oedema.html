What is Diabetic Macular Oedema?

Diabetic Macular Oedema (DMO) is a complication of diabetic retinopathy in which fluid leaks from damaged blood vessels into the macula — the central part of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision. It is the most common cause of vision loss in people with diabetes and affects approximately 1 in 10 people with diabetic retinopathy. DMO can occur at any stage of diabetic retinopathy.

How it affects vision

Central vision becomes blurred or distorted. Reading, recognising faces, and seeing fine detail become increasingly difficult. Vision may fluctuate — better on some days than others — due to changes in fluid levels. Unlike diabetic retinopathy itself, DMO directly and rapidly affects the vision used for everyday tasks.

Getting a diagnosis

Detected by OCT (optical coherence tomography) scanning, which shows fluid within the layers of the macula. Fluorescein angiography identifies leaking blood vessels. Annual NHS diabetic eye screening detects early changes before symptoms appear.

Who it affects

Anyone with Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes. Risk increases with longer duration of diabetes, poor blood sugar control, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and kidney disease. Pregnancy can also accelerate DMO in women with diabetes.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Faricimab (Vabysmo) — Fewer Injections

Dual-pathway injection. NICE approved for DMO. Up to every 4 months for some patients — far fewer clinic visits than older treatments.

Visit website ↗

✅ NHS Available

Aflibercept HD (Eylea HD)

High-dose 8mg aflibercept formulation. NICE considered it clinically equivalent to standard-dose Eylea (TA346) and recommended for routine commissioning without a separate appraisal. Allows extended dosing intervals — fewer injections than standard-dose Eylea for many patients.

Visit website ↗

🌍 Available Abroad

Sustained-Release Anti-VEGF Implants

A refillable implant placed in the eye during a one-time procedure delivers anti-VEGF medication continuously, with refills roughly every six months instead of frequent injections. The first such device, Susvimo (a port delivery system with ranibizumab), was approved by the US FDA for diabetic macular oedema in February 2025, based on a Phase 3 trial showing vision results comparable to monthly injections. It is not available on the NHS and is not licensed in the UK. The implant requires surgery and the device has had safety issues in the past, including an earlier recall and redesign, so it would not suit everyone. Other sustained-release approaches remain in trials.

🌅 Horizon

Gene Therapy for DMO

Single-injection gene therapy delivering continuous anti-VEGF production — potentially replacing all repeat injections permanently. Early-stage programmes building on wet AMD gene therapy research.

Practical guides

Never Miss Your Diabetes Eye Screening

Annual NHS diabetic eye screening is free and detects DMO before symptoms appear. Missing even one screening can allow DMO to progress to the point where treatment is less effective.

Understanding Your Injections

Anti-VEGF injections are given at your eye clinic under local anaesthetic. They are highly effective — most people see improvement or stabilisation within weeks. A loading phase of monthly injections is usually followed by less frequent maintenance doses.

Blood Sugar and Blood Pressure Control

Every 1% reduction in HbA1c reduces DMO progression risk significantly. Blood pressure below 130/80 also reduces risk. Work with your diabetes team on a CGM (continuous glucose monitor) if eligible — Libre 3 and Dexcom G7 are often NHS-funded.

Applying for Benefits

Vision loss from DMO supports a PIP claim. Diabetes UK has a dedicated benefits guide. RNIB (0303 123 9999) provides free help with forms.

Helpful technology

Blood Sugar

Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM)

A small sensor worn on the arm that tracks glucose continuously, helping maintain the blood-sugar control that directly slows DMO. Current NHS devices include FreeStyle Libre 2 Plus / Libre 3 Plus and Dexcom G7 (the older Dexcom G6 is being phased out from 2026). NHS-funded if you meet NICE eligibility (NG17 for type 1, NG28 for type 2 on insulin).

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

For the central reading loss DMO causes, optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers add contrast and colour modes, and larger desktop CCTV magnifiers suit longer reading.

AI Wearable

OrCam MyEye (reading & recognition camera)

Clips to glasses and reads printed and digital text aloud and recognises faces and products — useful when central vision makes reading difficult. Ray-Ban Meta glasses with Be My Eyes are a cheaper mainstream alternative for description and reading.

Free App

Seeing AI / Be My Eyes

Seeing AI reads text and identifies faces, products and currency through the camera; Be My Eyes connects to a sighted volunteer for live help. Both free, on iOS and Android.

Screen Settings

Larger Text, Contrast & Zoom

Built-in accessibility settings on phones, tablets and computers enlarge text, boost contrast and zoom the screen at no cost — often the simplest first step.

Benefits & registration

Free Annual NHS Eye Screening

Free for all people with diabetes. The single most important thing you can do to protect your vision.

PIP

Vision loss from DMO supports a PIP claim for both Daily Living and Mobility components. RNIB can help — 0303 123 9999.
