What is Retinal Vein Occlusion?

A retinal vein occlusion (RVO) is a blockage in a vein that drains blood from the retina, the light-sensitive tissue at the back of the eye. It usually affects one eye. When a small branch vein is blocked it is called a branch RVO (BRVO); when the main vein is blocked it is called a central RVO (CRVO). The resulting leakage and swelling can damage the macula, which handles central vision.

How it affects vision

Symptoms can include blurred vision in one eye, a dark spot, wavy or distorted vision, and difficulty reading or seeing detail. They can appear over hours or days. A branch RVO usually causes milder symptoms than a central RVO. If symptoms come on suddenly or are severe, or you see flashing lights, new floaters, or a shadow or curtain across your vision, go to an eye casualty or A&E straight away, because these can signal a retinal detachment, which needs urgent treatment.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosis is made after an eye examination, an OCT scan, fluorescein angiography (dye injected into the arm) and blood tests with a blood pressure check.

Who it affects

RVO mostly affects people over 65 and is linked to high blood pressure. Risk factors include high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, clotting problems, high BMI, an unhealthy diet and smoking. Sometimes no cause is found.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Anti-VEGF Injections

Injections into the eye reduce leakage and swelling at the macula and slow the growth of new blood vessels. They are repeated over several months. NICE has recommended ranibizumab and aflibercept for macular oedema after RVO (NICE guidance TA283, TA305 and TA409, applying in England; other UK nations have their own approval processes). Side effects are usually temporary, such as discomfort, redness, blurred vision and floaters.

✅ NHS Available

Laser and Steroid Implants

Laser can seal leaking vessels and is usually combined with injections. A steroid implant placed in the eye can reduce swelling and may be used in some people. Your eye specialist will explain which suits you.

✅ NHS Available

Monitoring Without Treatment

Not everyone needs treatment straight away. A branch RVO is more likely than a central RVO to improve on its own, so your clinic may monitor you first. Follow-up usually lasts about two years, and RVO can recur or affect the other eye, so regular checks continue after treatment.

Practical guides

Know the Emergency Signs

Sudden or severe sight loss, new floaters, flashing lights, or a shadow or curtain across your vision need same-day attention at an eye casualty or A&E. Do not wait to see if it settles.

Ask Your GP About Your General Health

RVO is linked to high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol and smoking. Ask your GP to check your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar, and for help to stop smoking if you smoke.

Expect Repeat Appointments

Injections are normally given at regular visits over several months, and treatment can take time to work. Keeping every appointment matters, because missed checks can mean missed swelling.

Watch Both Eyes

RVO can recur or affect the other eye. Covering each eye in turn now and then can help you notice a change. If you do, contact your eye clinic or optometrist promptly.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Living with Retinal Vein Occlusion

Driving

Many people can keep driving after RVO. The Macular Society says you must tell the DVLA only if both eyes are affected; the DVLA decides whether you meet the standard. Ask your optician or eye clinic how your own vision measures up.

Reading and Screen Use

Larger text, high contrast and dark mode, a magnifier and good lighting all help. Vision often improves as the brain adapts and the better eye takes over, so give treatment time to work.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
