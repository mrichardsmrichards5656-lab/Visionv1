What is Best Disease?

Best disease, also called Best vitelliform macular dystrophy, is an inherited condition that affects the macula — the central part of the retina used for reading, recognising faces and seeing fine detail. It is caused by changes in the BEST1 gene. Typically a yellow, yolk-like deposit forms at the macula in both eyes, which can later break up and leave scarring. Retina UK describes it as one of the more common inherited forms of macular degeneration, affecting around 1 in 10,000 people. Some people notice an effect on their sight in childhood; for others it is not noticed until later in life, and how much sight is affected varies widely, even within the same family.

How it affects vision

Central vision is affected, so reading, recognising faces and seeing fine detail can become harder, and straight lines may look distorted. Peripheral (side) vision is usually preserved, so most people can get around independently. Many people keep useful central vision for years, but it can decline gradually over time. A sudden drop in vision or new distortion can mean that abnormal blood vessels have grown beneath the macula (choroidal neovascularisation). This is uncommon, but it needs urgent assessment because it can be treated.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosis is made by an eye specialist, usually after a detailed eye examination and scans of the retina such as OCT and retinal photography. A test called an electro-oculogram (EOG), which measures how well the layer beneath the retina is working, is typically abnormal in Best disease and helps to confirm it. Genetic testing for BEST1 can confirm the diagnosis and is available on the NHS through inherited retinal disease clinics. Family members may also be examined and offered genetic counselling.

Who it affects

Best disease is usually inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, which means a person with one changed copy of the BEST1 gene has a 50% chance of passing it on to each child. Not everyone who carries the change is affected to the same degree, so relatives may carry the gene with few or no symptoms. A rarer recessive form also exists. Anyone who learns that they or their child have Best disease can ask for genetic counselling to understand what it means for the wider family.

Research & treatment progress

🔬 Pipeline

OPGx-BEST1 Gene Therapy (Opus Genetics)

A one-off injection under the retina that delivers a working copy of the BEST1 gene. The first human trial, BIRD-1 (Phase 1/2, around 10 people), dosed its first participant in November 2025, and the company reported positive early results in 2026, including 3- and 6-month data. This is very early work in a handful of people, aimed mainly at testing safety and looking for early signs of benefit. The trial sites are in the USA, so it is not available in the UK, and it is not yet known how well it works.

🌅 Horizon

Gene Editing and Stem Cell Research

Laboratory research is exploring other approaches, including using CRISPR gene editing to switch off the faulty copy of BEST1, and using stem cells to understand exactly how different BEST1 changes affect the retina. These are at an early research stage and are not treatments that anyone can have yet.

✅ NHS Available

Treating Complications (Abnormal Blood Vessels)

There is currently no treatment that slows or stops Best disease itself. If abnormal blood vessels grow beneath the macula, they can be treated with anti-VEGF injections, the same type of medicine used for wet AMD, which can help to protect vision. Laser or photodynamic therapy have also been used in some cases. This is why a sudden change in vision should always be checked promptly.

Practical guides

Ask for a Genetic Test

Genetic testing for BEST1 can confirm the diagnosis, helps relatives understand their own risk, and is the route into any gene-specific trials. Ask your ophthalmologist for a referral to an inherited retinal disease clinic. Testing is available on the NHS.

Know the Warning Signs

If your vision suddenly gets worse, or straight lines suddenly look newly wavy or distorted in one eye, contact your eye clinic or an optometrist straight away. Abnormal blood vessels beneath the macula are uncommon in Best disease but can be treated, and treatment works best when it is started early. Covering one eye at a time now and then can help you notice a change.

Tell Your Family

Because Best disease is usually inherited in a dominant pattern, each child of an affected person has a 50% chance of inheriting the gene change, and parents, brothers and sisters may carry it too, sometimes with few symptoms. Relatives can ask their GP or an eye specialist about an eye examination, and a genetics clinic can offer counselling and testing.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

Worth doing early rather than waiting until reading becomes difficult. Ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help. Electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded, though most clinics will let you try before buying. It is particularly useful when central vision is affected but side vision is preserved, because the right magnification and lighting can make a real difference.

Support at School

If your child is diagnosed, tell their school early. Large print, preferential seating and reduced copying from the board should be straightforward to arrange as reasonable adjustments, and your local authority's sensory support service can advise about a QTVI (Qualified Teacher of Children and Young People with Vision Impairment). Exam access arrangements such as extra time are not automatic — the school must evidence the need and show it is already your child's normal way of working, so raise it early. Your local SENDIASS gives free independent advice.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

For the central-vision reading difficulty, optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers (with contrast and colour modes) and larger desktop CCTV magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

AI Wearable

OrCam MyEye (reading & recognition camera)

Clips to any glasses frame and reads text aloud and recognises faces and products — useful when central vision makes reading tiring or difficult.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe people, products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task. Both are free to use.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Dark mode, high contrast, larger text and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and help with both glare and reduced acuity at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can and say clearly if you are due to start a job soon.

Living with Best Disease

Eccentric Viewing and Steady Eye Strategy

Two trainable techniques for central vision loss — often more useful than any gadget. Eccentric viewing means finding the healthiest part of your retina, slightly off-centre, and learning to look there instead of straight at things. Steady eye strategy is for reading: instead of moving your eyes along the line, you hold your gaze still and move the text through your best patch of vision. The evidence is mixed: earlier studies and a Macular Society review were positive, but the largest randomised trial (EFFECT, Moorfields, 200 people) found no significant benefit over standard low vision care. Many people still say it helps them, and it is free to try. It takes practice — usually a few sessions over a couple of months — and it feels odd at first. The Macular Society runs free one-to-one Skills for Seeing training plus a free online course: 0300 3030 111 or skills@macularsociety.org.

Reading and Screen Use

Central vision loss can make small print and faces harder to see. Larger text, high contrast and dark mode, a handheld or desktop magnifier, and good lighting all help. Many people find audiobooks and screen readers useful for long reading, while keeping print for short tasks.

Driving

Best disease affects people very differently, and many people can drive. The law requires you to tell the DVLA about eye conditions that affect both eyes, and the DVLA decides whether you meet the standard. Ask your ophthalmologist how your own vision measures up, and plan ahead for other ways of getting about.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
