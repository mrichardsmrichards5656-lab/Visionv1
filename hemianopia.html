Hemianopia

What is Hemianopia?

Hemianopia (also written hemianopsia) is the loss of half the visual field in both eyes simultaneously, caused by damage to the visual pathways inside the brain — not to the eyes themselves. The most common cause is stroke; others include brain tumours, traumatic brain injury, and other neurological events. In homonymous hemianopia — the most common type — the same side of the visual field is lost in both eyes, because both pathways are damaged at the same point. In bitemporal hemianopia, the outer edges of vision in both eyes are lost, typically due to a pituitary tumour pressing on the optic chiasm. Hemianopia is estimated to affect more than 230,000 people in the UK.

How it affects vision

The impact depends on which side of the visual field is affected, but common difficulties include: reading, because words fall off one side of the page and disappear into the blind field; walking into objects, door frames, and people on the blind side; crossing roads safely; and navigating busy environments. Driving must stop immediately upon diagnosis — there is a legal duty to notify the DVLA. Many people are initially unaware of the loss, as the brain partly compensates, but formal testing makes it clear. A dark 'curtain' or shadow on one side of vision, or persistent difficulty seeing on one side, should prompt urgent assessment.

Getting a diagnosis

Hemianopia is detected with a formal visual field test — either a confrontation test (a quick bedside test where the examiner moves a finger into different areas of your vision) or automated perimetry (Humphrey or Goldmann). Brain imaging (MRI or CT) identifies the underlying cause. For DVLA driving assessment purposes, a binocular Esterman visual field test is used. If you have had a stroke and nobody has formally assessed your visual fields, specifically ask the stroke team for a neuro-ophthalmology or orthoptic referral.

Who it affects

Homonymous hemianopia — loss of the same side in both eyes — is estimated to affect more than 230,000 people in the UK, the majority following stroke. It occurs when the visual pathway is damaged after the optic chiasm, where the nerve fibres from both eyes cross. Bitemporal hemianopia (loss of the outer fields in both eyes) is usually caused by a pituitary tumour pressing on the chiasm at the crossing point. Quadrantanopia — loss of a quarter rather than a half of the visual field — is a partial form. In all cases, the eyes themselves are healthy — the problem is in the brain's visual pathway.

Research & treatment progress

🔬 Experimental

Vision Restoration Therapy (VRT)

VRT uses computer-based training to stimulate the border region between the seeing and non-seeing visual field. A 2021 Oxford University study (Ajina et al., Journal of Neuroscience) found that VRT was associated with increased visual sensitivity in the affected area and measurable changes in brain activity on fMRI. Two important caveats: the study involved only seven people, and it required around 25 minutes of training every day for three to six months, with improvements largely limited to the specific area trained. It remains experimental — not a standard NHS treatment — and results vary significantly between individuals. Research into visual restoration continues.

🔬 In Use

Prism Glasses — Sector and Fresnel Prisms

Prism lenses bend light from the blind side into the seeing visual field, effectively widening the useful visual area. They do not restore lost vision but reduce the frequency of collisions and improve navigation. Two types are used: sector prisms (fitted to the outer edge of the lens) and Fresnel prisms (a thin adhesive sheet applied to existing lenses). Both require fitting by a low vision specialist or orthoptist and a period of adaptation. Referral to a low vision clinic or orthoptics department is the starting point.

Practical guides

Notify the DVLA Immediately

If you are diagnosed with homonymous or bitemporal hemianopia, you must stop driving at once and notify the DVLA. This is a legal requirement — continuing to drive is a criminal offence and invalidates your insurance. Your licence will normally be revoked. After at least 12 months, if the underlying cause is stable (stroke qualifies), you may be considered for the 'exceptional cases' route — which requires clinical confirmation that you have fully adapted, plus a formal practical assessment in a dual-control vehicle. Be realistic about the numbers: in a UK study of 144 stroke survivors with hemianopia, only about a third were even eligible to be considered, and only 13 went forward for DVLA referral — though of the 12 who were assessed, all passed and returned to driving. Overall pass rates for this route are not published. It is a genuine pathway for a minority of well-adapted people, not a routine expectation. Ask your neurologist or ophthalmologist whether it is realistic in your case.

Scanning Training — Learning to Look

The most consistently effective rehabilitation for hemianopia is scanning training: learning to systematically move your eyes towards the blind side to compensate for the missing field. A vision rehabilitation specialist or orthoptist can teach this. It significantly improves reading, navigation, and daily safety. This is available through NHS neurological rehabilitation services — if it has not been offered after a stroke, ask specifically for an orthoptic or low vision referral.

Reading After Hemianopia

Reading is often the most frustrating daily difficulty. Strategies that help: rotating the page so text falls in the seeing field; using a finger or ruler to track along lines; using text-to-speech on your phone or computer (available free on both iOS and Android); or requesting large print or audiobooks. RNIB Talking Books are free and need no screen at all.

Registering as Sight Impaired

Hemianopia may qualify for a Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI) depending on the severity of field loss. A CVI opens access to local authority rehabilitation services, free NHS sight tests, a Disabled Person's Railcard and travel concessions; a council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic. Those registered severely sight impaired can also claim Blind Person's Allowance and a 50% reduction on their TV licence. Ask the consultant managing your care — neurologist, neuro-ophthalmologist, or ophthalmologist — about certification.

Safety at Home and Outside

A vision rehabilitation officer can work with you on safe outdoor routes, road-crossing techniques, and navigating crowds. On the blind side: remove trip hazards at home; sit with your blind side to a wall in social situations; approach junctions and road crossings especially carefully. A symbol cane signals your visual difficulty to others. If you had a stroke, your rehabilitation team should include visual rehabilitation — ask specifically if it has not been offered.

Helpful technology

Reading Aid

Line Guides & Typoscopes

A typoscope is a simple card with a slot that reveals one line of text at a time, helping with the losing-your-place problem common in hemianopia. Reading rulers work the same way — cheap, low-tech, and surprisingly effective.

Prism Lenses

Peli Prism Glasses

Specialist prism lenses (such as the Peli prism design) shift an image from the blind side into the seeing field, expanding awareness of obstacles. Fitted by a low vision optometrist or orthoptist after assessment. They take practice to adapt to.

Free App

Seeing AI & Text-to-Speech

When reading becomes tiring, free text-to-speech turns any document into audio. Seeing AI (free, Microsoft) reads printed text aloud through the phone camera; the built-in Read Aloud on iPhone and Android reads on-screen text.

Navigation

Smartphone Navigation Apps

Apps like Google Maps and BlindSquare give spoken, turn-by-turn directions, reducing reliance on spotting visual landmarks on the blind side. Useful for rebuilding travel confidence after stroke.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Hemianopia significantly affects daily activities and the ability to travel safely. PIP assesses functional impact — difficulty reading, navigating, crossing roads, and the risk of accidents all strengthen a claim. RNIB and Citizens Advice can help with the form.

Blue Badge

If hemianopia affects your ability to walk safely and independently in public — particularly following stroke with additional mobility difficulties — you may qualify for a Blue Badge. Apply via your local council.

Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI)

If field loss is severe enough to meet CVI criteria, your consultant can issue one. This unlocks local authority rehabilitation services, possible council tax reduction, and — for registered severely sight impaired people — a 50% reduction on the TV licence.

Access to Work

If you are working, Access to Work can fund adjustments such as screen-reading software, adapted lighting, or travel costs if driving is no longer possible.

Living with Hemianopia

The First Weeks

A sudden hemianopia — particularly after stroke — is disorientating and frightening. Some visual field may recover in the weeks immediately after a stroke as swelling reduces, but field loss that remains at six months is typically permanent. Grief for lost vision and the driving licence is real and valid. RNIB Sight Loss Counselling (0303 123 9999) is available free of charge and offers confidential support from specialists in sight loss.

Safe at Home

Arrange your home so that important things — keys, phone, clock — are on your seeing side. Good, consistent lighting helps maximise what your remaining vision can do. Remove trip hazards, particularly on the blind side. In social settings, sit with your blind side towards a wall so conversations happen in your seeing field.

Driving After Hemianopia

Losing a driving licence is one of the most significant practical consequences of hemianopia. It is not necessarily permanent: the DVLA exceptional cases pathway exists for people who have adapted well after at least 12 months. In the meantime, plan alternatives: Passenger Assist on trains, a free bus pass with a CVI, community transport schemes, and Access to Work for work journeys. See the Driving and DVLA section under Getting Around for more detail.

Telling People

Many people with hemianopia look completely well and others don't understand why they walk into things or struggle with reading. A short explanation — 'I can't see anything on my left side' — usually helps. A sunflower lanyard or sight loss card can signal to others that you may need a little extra awareness.
