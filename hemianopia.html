Hemianopia

Hemianopia is the loss of half the visual field in both eyes, caused by damage to the visual pathways inside the brain — most commonly after a stroke.

What is Hemianopia?

Hemianopia (also written hemianopsia) is the loss of half the visual field in both eyes simultaneously, caused by damage to the visual pathways inside the brain — not to the eyes themselves. The most common cause is stroke; others include brain tumours and traumatic brain injury. In homonymous hemianopia — the most common type — the same side of the visual field is lost in both eyes. In bitemporal hemianopia, the outer edges of vision in both eyes are lost, typically due to a pituitary tumour pressing on the optic chiasm. Hemianopia is estimated to affect more than 230,000 people in the UK.

How it affects vision

The impact depends on which side is affected, but common difficulties include reading (words fall off one side of the page and disappear), walking into objects and people on the blind side, crossing roads safely, and navigating busy environments. Driving must stop immediately on diagnosis — there is a legal duty to notify the DVLA. Many people are initially unaware of the loss, as the brain partly compensates, but formal testing makes it clear. A dark 'curtain' or shadow on one side, or persistent difficulty seeing on one side, should prompt urgent assessment.

Getting a diagnosis

Hemianopia is detected with a formal visual field test — either a confrontation test (a quick bedside test) or automated perimetry (Humphrey or Goldmann). Brain imaging (MRI or CT) identifies the underlying cause. For DVLA driving assessment, a binocular Esterman visual field test is used. If you have had a stroke and nobody has formally assessed your visual fields, specifically ask the stroke team for a neuro-ophthalmology or orthoptic referral.

Who it affects

Homonymous hemianopia — loss of the same side in both eyes — is estimated to affect more than 230,000 people in the UK, the majority following stroke. It occurs when the visual pathway is damaged after the optic chiasm, where the nerve fibres from both eyes cross. Bitemporal hemianopia (loss of the outer fields in both eyes) is usually caused by a pituitary tumour. Quadrantanopia — loss of a quarter rather than a half — is a partial form. In all cases the eyes themselves are healthy; the problem is in the brain's visual pathway.

Research & treatment progress

🔬 Experimental

Vision Restoration Therapy (VRT)

VRT uses computer-based training to stimulate the border between the seeing and non-seeing field. A 2021 Oxford University study (Ajina et al., Journal of Neuroscience) found VRT was associated with increased visual sensitivity and measurable changes in brain activity on fMRI. However, it remains experimental — not yet a standard NHS treatment — and results vary significantly between individuals.

🔬 In Use

Prism Glasses — Sector and Fresnel Prisms

Prism lenses bend light from the blind side into the seeing field, effectively widening the useful visual area. They do not restore lost vision but reduce collisions and improve navigation. Two types are used: sector prisms (fitted to the lens edge) and Fresnel prisms (a thin adhesive sheet). Both require fitting by a low vision specialist or orthoptist and a period of adaptation.

Practical guides

Notify the DVLA Immediately

If you are diagnosed with homonymous or bitemporal hemianopia, you must stop driving at once and notify the DVLA. This is a legal requirement — continuing to drive is a criminal offence and invalidates your insurance. Your licence will normally be revoked. After at least 12 months, if the underlying cause is stable (stroke qualifies), you may be considered for the 'exceptional cases' driving assessment — a formal test in a dual-control vehicle. UK clinical studies have found that most people referred through this pathway pass. Ask your neurologist or ophthalmologist about it.

Scanning Training — Learning to Look

The most consistently effective rehabilitation for hemianopia is scanning training: learning to systematically move your eyes towards the blind side to compensate for the missing field. A vision rehabilitation specialist or orthoptist can teach this. It significantly improves reading, navigation, and daily safety. If it has not been offered after a stroke, ask specifically for an orthoptic or low vision referral.

Reading After Hemianopia

Reading is often the most frustrating daily difficulty. Strategies that help: rotating the page so text falls in the seeing field; using a finger or ruler to track lines; using text-to-speech on your phone or computer (free on both iOS and Android); or requesting large print or audiobooks. RNIB Talking Books are free and need no screen at all.

Registering as Sight Impaired

Hemianopia may qualify for a Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI) depending on the severity of field loss. A CVI unlocks local authority rehabilitation services, possible council tax reduction, and — for those registered severely sight impaired — a free TV licence. Ask the consultant managing your care.

Helpful technology

Reading Aid

Line Guides & Typoscopes

A typoscope is a simple card with a slot that reveals one line of text at a time, helping with the losing-your-place problem common in hemianopia. Reading rulers work the same way — cheap, low-tech, and surprisingly effective.

Prism Lenses

Peli Prism Glasses

Specialist prism lenses (such as the Peli prism design) shift an image from the blind side into the seeing field, expanding awareness of obstacles. Fitted by a low vision optometrist or orthoptist after assessment. They take practice to adapt to.

Free App

Seeing AI & Text-to-Speech

When reading becomes tiring, free text-to-speech turns any document into audio. Seeing AI (free, Microsoft) reads printed text aloud through the phone camera; the built-in Read Aloud on iPhone and Android reads on-screen text.

Navigation

Smartphone Navigation Apps

Apps like Google Maps and BlindSquare give spoken, turn-by-turn directions, reducing reliance on spotting visual landmarks on the blind side. Useful for rebuilding travel confidence after stroke.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Hemianopia significantly affects daily activities and the ability to travel safely. PIP assesses functional impact — difficulty reading, navigating, crossing roads, and the risk of accidents all strengthen a claim. RNIB and Citizens Advice can help with the form.

Blue Badge

If hemianopia affects your ability to walk safely and independently — particularly following stroke with additional mobility difficulties — you may qualify for a Blue Badge. Apply via your local council.

Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI)

If field loss is severe enough to meet CVI criteria, your consultant can issue one. This unlocks local authority rehabilitation services, possible council tax reduction, and — for registered severely sight impaired people — a free TV licence.
