What is Optic Neuritis?

Optic Neuritis is inflammation of the optic nerve — the nerve that carries visual information from the eye to the brain. It causes sudden vision loss, usually in one eye, often accompanied by pain on eye movement. It is strongly associated with multiple sclerosis (MS) — approximately 50% of people with optic neuritis will go on to develop MS. It can also occur in isolation or in association with other conditions including neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD).

How it affects vision

Sudden blurring or loss of vision in one eye, typically developing over hours to days. Pain behind the eye, worsened by eye movement. Reduced colour vision — red colours appear washed out or faded. In most cases, vision recovers significantly within weeks to months, though some permanent reduction may remain. The Uhthoff phenomenon — temporary worsening of vision with heat or exercise — is common.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosed by an ophthalmologist or neurologist based on symptoms, visual field testing, colour vision testing, and OCT scanning of the optic nerve. MRI brain and spinal cord scanning is routinely performed to assess MS risk. VEP (visual evoked potential) testing measures the speed of signals along the optic nerve.

Who it affects

Most commonly affects women aged 20–45. Associated with MS in approximately 50% of cases. Can also be associated with neuromyelitis optica (NMO), a more severe condition requiring different treatment. Optic neuritis can recur.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

IV Methylprednisolone — Speeds Recovery

High-dose IV steroids speed recovery from acute optic neuritis but do not improve final vision outcome. Standard care in UK hospitals. The goal is returning to work and activity faster — not improving the ceiling of recovery.

🔬 Pipeline

Neuroprotection — Preventing Permanent Damage

Researchers are testing whether drugs given during an acute attack can protect optic nerve fibres from permanent loss. A UK phase 2 trial of phenytoin (Lancet Neurology, 2016) found about a 30% reduction in nerve-fibre-layer loss versus placebo. Important caveat: this and similar trials (memantine, erythropoietin) protected the nerve's structure but have not yet been shown to improve actual vision — whether that translates into better eyesight is still the key unanswered question, and none is an approved treatment.

🔬 Pipeline

MS Disease-Modifying Therapies

For people who develop MS following optic neuritis, disease-modifying therapies (DMTs) significantly reduce the risk of further relapses and further optic nerve damage. Early treatment decisions after a first episode are important — discuss with your neurologist.

Practical guides

Seek Urgent Assessment

Sudden vision loss in one eye is always a medical emergency. Go to A&E or an urgent eye clinic immediately. Do not wait for a GP appointment. Early assessment determines whether MS-related treatment is needed.

Understanding the MS Link

Being told you have optic neuritis and an MS risk can be frightening. Ask your neurologist for a clear explanation of your MRI results and what they mean for your specific risk. MS Society (ms-society.org.uk) has excellent information.

Managing Uhthoff Phenomenon

Avoid overheating — hot baths, saunas, and intense exercise can temporarily worsen vision. Use cooling vests, fans, and lukewarm rather than hot water. Vision returns to baseline once temperature normalises.

Driving and Optic Neuritis

You must inform the DVLA if you have had optic neuritis. Driving is not permitted during an acute episode. Your ophthalmologist or neurologist will advise when it is safe to return to driving.

Helpful technology

Monitoring

Colour & Contrast Self-Checks

Vision usually recovers over weeks to months, but colour (especially red) and contrast can lag. Free apps like Colour Blind Pal, or simply comparing each eye on a coloured object day to day, help you track recovery and spot any recurrence to report to your eye team.

Lighting & Contrast

Lighting & High-Contrast Settings

While contrast sensitivity recovers, good task lighting plus high-contrast and larger-text settings on devices make reading and screens more comfortable. Built into all phones, tablets and computers at no cost.

Low-Vision Aids

Magnification (Only If Vision Doesn't Fully Recover)

Most people regain useful vision, so aids are rarely needed long-term. If a deficit remains, a free NHS low-vision assessment can provide magnifiers and advice tailored to your remaining vision.

Support

MS Society Resources

Optic neuritis can be an early sign of multiple sclerosis. If MS is diagnosed or suspected, the MS Society offers comprehensive information, a helpline and local support.

Benefits & registration

PIP

Vision loss and associated fatigue or disability from optic neuritis or MS supports a PIP claim. Both Daily Living and Mobility components may apply.

MS Society Benefits Advice

If MS is associated, the MS Society has a dedicated benefits advice service — mssociety.org.uk.

Access to Work

If optic neuritis or associated MS affects your ability to work, Access to Work covers equipment and support. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work.
