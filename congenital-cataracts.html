What is Congenital Cataracts?

A cataract is a clouding of the lens inside the eye. Congenital cataracts are present at birth, while developmental (or childhood) cataracts appear during childhood. The lens is normally clear and helps focus light onto the retina — when it becomes cloudy, vision is blocked. Congenital cataracts affect approximately 2.5 per 10,000 infants under one year of age in the UK. They can affect one eye (unilateral) or both eyes (bilateral). They are uncommon but regularly seen in UK paediatric ophthalmology clinics, and a large proportion have a genetic cause.

How it affects vision

Dense cataracts in babies prevent normal visual development. If not treated early, they can cause permanent changes in the visual part of the brain (amblyopia, or 'lazy eye'), leading to reduced vision even after the cataract is removed. This is why early surgery is so important — ideally before three months of age, or even younger for cataracts in one eye. Mild cataracts may not affect vision and only need monitoring with glasses. After surgery, children need glasses or contact lenses to focus, because the natural lens has been removed.

Getting a diagnosis

Congenital cataracts are often detected during the NHS newborn and infant physical examination (NIPE) within 72 hours of birth, or at the 6-8 week check. The 'red reflex' test is key — a cataract can stop the normal red reflection from the back of the eye. A baby who screens positive should be seen by an ophthalmologist within two weeks. Further tests include a detailed eye examination under anaesthetic and genetic testing, as a large proportion of cases are inherited.

Who it affects

Congenital cataracts affect boys and girls. Causes include: an inherited genetic change passed from a parent, other genetic conditions (such as Down's syndrome), and infections caught by the mother during pregnancy (such as rubella and chickenpox). In many cases the exact cause cannot be identified. Because a large proportion are genetic, genetic testing and counselling are often recommended, and some cases are associated with other health conditions.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Early Cataract Surgery

Surgery to remove the cloudy lens is the established treatment and is highly effective when done early. For babies under one year, a lensectomy with posterior capsulotomy and anterior vitrectomy is typically performed. Unilateral cataracts are usually operated around six weeks of age, bilateral around ten weeks, to protect visual development.

✅ NHS Available

Newborn Screening (NIPE)

The UK National Screening Committee recommends all eligible newborns are offered eye screening within 72 hours of birth and again at 6-8 weeks. This early detection of congenital cataracts is critical — the earlier a dense cataract is found and removed, the better the visual outcome.

🔬 Pipeline

Intraocular Lens (IOL) Research

Research continues into the best timing and method of implanting an artificial lens (IOL) in young children. The Infant Aphakia Treatment Study (unilateral cataract) found contact lenses and primary lens implants gave similar vision at five years, but babies who received an implant needed significantly more additional eye operations — so for infants under one year, contact lenses are generally preferred. The findings help surgeons tailor treatment to each child.

🌅 Horizon

Genetic Research

A large proportion of congenital cataracts are genetic. Researchers are identifying the genes responsible (including EPHA2, which is also linked to microphthalmia), improving diagnosis and genetic counselling for families and laying groundwork for future therapies.

Practical guides

Act Quickly on Diagnosis

For dense cataracts, timing is critical. Surgery before three months of age gives the best chance of good vision. If your baby is diagnosed, make sure you understand the recommended timeline and keep every appointment.

Understand Post-Surgery Care

After cataract removal, your child will need glasses or contact lenses to focus, and often patching of the stronger eye to prevent amblyopia. Compliance with this is the single biggest factor in a good outcome — it is demanding but vital.

Ask About Glaucoma Risk

Children who have had cataract surgery have a lifelong increased risk of glaucoma (raised eye pressure). Make sure your child has regular monitoring, even into adulthood.

Genetic Testing

Ask for a referral for genetic testing and counselling. Many congenital cataracts are inherited, and understanding the cause helps with family planning and identifying any associated conditions.

Educational Support

Request a QTVI (Qualified Teacher of Visual Impairment) from your local authority. Even children with good outcomes after surgery may need some support, large print, or extra time.

Emotional Support for Parents

A diagnosis and early surgery for your baby is stressful. Lean on RNIB's helpline (0303 123 9999) and peer support groups — you are not alone, and outcomes for early-treated children are often very good.

Helpful technology

Vision

Contact Lenses for Infants

After cataract surgery in babies, specialist contact lenses are often preferred over implanted lenses (the IATS study found implanted lenses in under-1s led to more follow-up operations). Fitted and managed by the eye clinic, who teach parents to insert and care for them.

Vision

Glasses (often bifocal)

Children who have had cataract surgery usually need glasses — sometimes bifocals — to focus at different distances. Free on the NHS for under-16s, and worth choosing sturdy, flexible frames for young children.

Treatment

Patching for Amblyopia

Patching the stronger eye treats the 'lazy eye' (amblyopia) that often follows childhood cataract, training the brain to use the operated eye. Decorated or fabric patches make this easier for young children to accept.

Education

Low-Vision & Learning Support

If some visual deficit remains, magnifiers, high-contrast low-clutter materials, good lighting and a QTVI (qualified teacher of vision impairment) support learning. Arranged via the low-vision clinic and local sensory education team.

Access

Built-in Device Accessibility

Tablets and computers used at home and school have free built-in tools — larger text, high contrast, zoom, and (where needed) VoiceOver/TalkBack screen readers — to make schoolwork accessible.

Benefits & registration

Disability Living Allowance (DLA)

Children under 16 with significant vision impairment from congenital cataracts may be eligible for DLA. Call RNIB (0303 123 9999) for free help with the form. Apply early — backdating is not available.

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

If your child's vision remains impaired, ask the ophthalmologist about registration as sight impaired or severely sight impaired, which unlocks further support.

Free NHS Eye Care and Glasses

All under-16s receive free NHS sight tests and optical vouchers for glasses or contact lenses. Children who have had cataract surgery need lifelong monitoring, which is free on the NHS.

Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP)

An EHCP may be appropriate for support in school. It is not automatic — the local authority must agree to assess and then to issue a plan, with a right of appeal if refused. Contact your local authority's SEND team, ask about a QTVI assessment, and get free independent advice from your local SENDIASS.
