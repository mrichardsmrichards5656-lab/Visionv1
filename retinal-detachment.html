Retinal Detachment

Retinal detachment is a medical emergency in which the retina peels away from the back of the eye. The warning signs — sudden floaters, flashes, or a shadow — need same-day A&E assessment.

What is Retinal Detachment?

Retinal detachment is a medical emergency. The retina — the light-sensitive lining at the back of the eye — peels away from the underlying tissue that supplies it with oxygen and nutrients. Without prompt surgery, the cells in the detached area can be permanently damaged and vision lost. There are three main types: rhegmatogenous (the most common), caused by a tear or hole in the retina; tractional, caused by scar tissue pulling the retina away (most often in diabetic retinopathy); and exudative, caused by fluid building up beneath the retina. Approximately 85% of retinal detachments can be repaired with a single operation.

How it affects vision

The warning signs of a retinal detachment are distinctive and must be taken seriously: a sudden shower of new floaters (dark spots, threads, or cobwebs); flashes of light, especially in the dark or periphery; and — most urgently — a dark shadow or 'curtain' that appears to move across part of your vision. If any of these appear, especially in combination, go to your nearest A&E eye unit immediately. Do not wait to see your GP. Do not drive yourself. Every hour before surgery increases the risk of permanent damage. If the detachment reaches the macula, central vision is at greater risk.

Getting a diagnosis

Retinal detachment is diagnosed with a dilated eye examination: the doctor puts drops in your eye to widen the pupil, then examines the retina directly. An ultrasound scan may be used if the view is obscured. In A&E this should happen the same day. If detachment is confirmed, surgery is arranged urgently. Macula-on detachments — where central vision is still intact — are operated on within 24 hours to protect it. Macula-off cases are still urgent and usually operated on within a few days.

Who it affects

Retinal detachment affects around 10,000 people in the UK each year. Risk factors include high short-sightedness (myopia), previous cataract surgery, a family history of retinal detachment, previous detachment in the other eye, trauma, and advancing age. Many people experience a posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) first — a common, usually harmless ageing process. PVD causes floaters and flashes and rarely leads to detachment, but any new floaters or flashes should be assessed by an optometrist the same day to rule out a retinal tear.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Vitrectomy, Scleral Buckle, and Pneumatic Retinopexy

Around 85% of retinal detachments are successfully repaired with a single operation. The main approaches are: vitrectomy (most common) — the vitreous gel is removed and a gas or silicone oil bubble placed inside the eye to support the retina; scleral buckle — a soft silicone band is stitched around the outside of the eye to close the tear; and pneumatic retinopexy — a gas bubble injected in the clinic for selected uncomplicated detachments. Some cases require a combination or a second operation.

🔬 Pipeline

Air vs Gas Tamponade After Vitrectomy — UK Multi-Centre Trial

Current UK research is comparing air versus fluorinated gas as the tamponade agent after vitrectomy. Gas provides longer support but requires significant restrictions (no flying, specific positioning, longer recovery). If air proves equally effective, recovery would be considerably simpler. A 10-centre, non-inferiority trial led by Liverpool University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust is under way, with a 24-week primary outcome.

Practical guides

THIS IS AN EMERGENCY — Go Immediately

If you notice a sudden shower of new floaters, flashes of light, or a shadow or curtain moving across your vision — go to your nearest hospital eye unit or A&E right now. Call ahead if you can. Do not drive yourself — call a taxi or ask someone to take you. Do not wait until morning to see your GP. Every hour before treatment increases the risk of permanent vision loss.

You Must Not Fly After Gas Bubble Surgery

If your surgery uses a gas bubble (vitrectomy or pneumatic retinopexy), you must not travel by aircraft or ascend to significant altitude until the gas has completely absorbed — typically six to ten weeks. The change in atmospheric pressure causes the gas to expand dangerously. Also inform any anaesthetist about the gas bubble before any operation under general anaesthetic.

Positioning After Surgery

Your surgeon may ask you to position yourself face-down, or to lie on a specific side, for a period after surgery to keep the gas bubble in contact with the area of detachment. Follow the instructions exactly — even a few hours of incorrect positioning can affect the outcome.

Watch the Other Eye

After a retinal detachment, your other eye carries a higher risk. Your ophthalmologist will examine it carefully. Learn the warning signs — new floaters, flashes, or a shadow. If any appear in the other eye, seek emergency assessment immediately.

Helpful technology

Monitoring

Phone Magnifier & Reminders

During recovery, the built-in magnifier and high-contrast modes on your phone (Settings then Accessibility) help with reading while vision is blurry from the gas bubble. Reminder apps help track the strict post-surgery eye-drop schedule.

Low Vision

Desktop Video Magnifier (CCTV)

If central vision is reduced after a macula-off detachment, a desktop video magnifier enlarges text and photos with adjustable contrast. Available free through an NHS low vision clinic — ask your ophthalmologist for a referral.

Free App

Seeing AI & Be My Eyes

While vision is recovering, Seeing AI (free, Microsoft) reads text and describes scenes through the camera, and Be My Eyes connects you by live video to a sighted volunteer who can help with everyday tasks.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

If retinal detachment has caused lasting vision impairment, you may be eligible for PIP based on the functional impact on daily life and mobility. You can apply during recovery — don't wait for final visual outcomes.

Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI)

If surgery leaves you with significant permanent vision loss — particularly if the macula was detached — your consultant ophthalmologist can issue a CVI. This unlocks local authority rehabilitation services and, for registered severely sight impaired people, a free TV licence.

Sick Pay and Work Adjustments

Recovery requires at least two weeks off work, and longer for physically active or visually demanding roles. If there are lasting vision changes, Access to Work can fund workplace adaptations and the Equality Act 2010 requires your employer to make reasonable adjustments.
