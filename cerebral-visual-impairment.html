What is Cerebral Visual Impairment?

Cerebral Visual Impairment (CVI) is the most common cause of visual impairment in children in the developed world, caused by damage to the visual processing parts of the brain — not the eyes themselves. The eyes are often healthy. CVI typically results from brain injury around birth, stroke, or brain malformation.

How it affects vision

CVI presents very differently from eye-based conditions. A child may appear to 'look through' objects, have better peripheral than central vision, or seem visually inattentive. Visual fatigue is common. Features include difficulty with visual clutter, better vision for familiar objects, light-gazing, and better responses to bright colours.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosed by a specialist paediatric ophthalmologist or neuro-ophthalmologist. Standard visual acuity tests may underestimate functional vision. Brain MRI identifies the area of brain damage. A specialist CVI assessment is the gold standard.

Who it affects

The most common cause of childhood visual impairment in developed countries. An estimated 30–40% of children with cerebral palsy have CVI. Can affect people of any age following stroke or brain injury.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Active Learning and Early Intervention

Structured approaches that use the brain's ability to adapt (neuroplasticity) to help children make the most of their vision. The most widely used framework is Christine Roman-Lantzy's CVI Range assessment and intervention approach. In the UK, CVI Scotland — led by Professor Gordon Dutton, a paediatric ophthalmologist — provides free, detailed guidance for families and teachers. Little Bear Sees is a separate US parent-founded charity offering practical tips and apps. Evidence for these approaches comes mainly from small studies and clinical experience rather than large randomised trials, but early, individualised support is widely recommended by specialists.

Practical guides

Request a Specialist CVI Assessment

Standard eye charts don't capture the complexity of CVI. Ask for a functional vision assessment using the CVI Range or equivalent specialist tool.

Reduce Visual Clutter

Children with CVI often see best when objects are presented one at a time against a plain background. Declutter desk surfaces and reduce busy wall displays near the child's workspace.

Use Preferred Colours and Movement

Bright, saturated single colours often elicit stronger visual responses, and red and yellow are the most commonly reported preferences — but this varies between children. Identify your child's preferred colour, then use it consistently in learning materials and on personal belongings.

Getting the Right Support for Your Child

Children with CVI may need an EHC plan, input from a Qualified Teacher of Children and Young People with Vision Impairment (QTVI), and specialist technology. These are not automatic: an EHC plan requires the local authority to agree to a needs assessment and then to issue a plan, and both stages can be refused — though you have a right of appeal to the SEND Tribunal. A QTVI referral usually comes through the local authority sensory support service. Contact your local authority, ask your SENCo about a QTVI, and get free independent advice from your local SENDIASS. The VIEW charity and CVI Scotland also have guidance for families.

Helpful technology

High Contrast

High-Contrast, Decluttered Displays

CVI is a brain-based difficulty processing what the eyes see, so reducing visual clutter matters more than magnifying. High-contrast mode, dark backgrounds, and showing one simple item at a time (rather than busy screens) help the brain interpret an image. Built into all devices for free.

Educational

Low-Clutter, High-Contrast Materials

Simple, uncluttered, high-contrast learning resources — single objects on plain backgrounds, consistent placement, and favourite colours (often red or yellow) — designed for how a child with CVI sees. Available from RNIB and specialist education suppliers.

Environment

Lighting, Positioning & Movement Cues

Controlling glare, using backlighting (e.g. a light box or tablet) to make objects 'pop', presenting items in the child's best visual field, and using movement or shine to attract attention are core CVI strategies. A QTVI (qualified teacher of vision impairment) can advise.

Communication

AAC & Switch-Accessible Apps

For children who also have complex needs, accessible communication (AAC) apps and switch-accessible tablet software present choices in a clear, CVI-friendly way. Built-in switch control on iPad/Android is free; dedicated AAC apps vary.

Screen Reader

Screen Readers (as needed)

Where vision is very limited, built-in VoiceOver (Apple) and TalkBack (Android) provide spoken access at no cost — sometimes combined with visual access depending on the child's profile.

Benefits & registration

Education, Health and Care (EHC) Plan

An EHC plan can fund specialist education, QTVI input and specialist technology. It is not automatic — the local authority must agree to assess and then to issue a plan, and both stages can be refused, with a right of appeal. Your local SENDIASS gives free independent advice.

DLA for Children / PIP for Adults

The visual and neurological impact of CVI supports a claim at higher rates. RNIB can help — 0303 123 9999.

Carer's Allowance

If you provide 35+ hours of care per week, you may be entitled to Carer's Allowance (£86.45/week from April 2026).

Living with Cerebral Visual Impairment

Reducing Visual Clutter

Children and adults with CVI see best when their environment is simplified. At home: plain tablecloths, one object at a time on surfaces, reduced wall decoration near work areas. At school: a clear desk, plain exercise books, and a distraction-reduced workspace make a significant difference.

Using Colour and Movement

Many people with CVI respond better to bright, saturated single colours, and red and yellow are the most commonly reported preferences — but this is individual, and some children have a clear preference for a different colour entirely. Work out which colour your child responds to, then use it consistently in learning materials and on personal belongings. Gentle movement (such as a slowly rotating object) can attract visual attention when static objects are not noticed.

Managing Visual Fatigue

Visual processing is effortful for people with CVI — far more so than for those with eye-based conditions. Build in rest breaks from visually demanding tasks. Recognise when vision 'switches off' — this is not laziness, it is neurological fatigue requiring rest.

Technology and Screens

High contrast display settings, large text, and simplified screen layouts help significantly. iPads with high contrast mode are commonly used by children with CVI. Apps should be simple, uncluttered, and use high contrast — many standard apps are too visually complex.

Working with Schools

CVI is still widely misunderstood in mainstream education. Many teachers have never heard of it. CVI Scotland (cviscotland.org) provides free training resources for teachers. The EHC plan should specify CVI explicitly and list concrete environmental modifications as legal requirements.

For Parents — Cerebral Visual Impairment

CVI is the most common cause of childhood visual impairment in the UK. It is widely misunderstood — even by schools and some professionals. This section gives you the key information.

CVI is a Brain Condition, Not an Eye Condition

The eyes in CVI are often completely healthy. The difficulty is in how the brain processes visual information. This means standard eye tests significantly underestimate your child's visual difficulties — and it means that with the right stimulation and environment, vision can improve over time as the brain adapts.

Get a Specialist CVI Assessment

Ask for a functional vision assessment using the CVI Range (Roman-Lantzy) tool — not just a standard eye test. This tells you and the school exactly how your child sees and what environments and approaches help most.

Schools Often Don't Know About CVI

Many mainstream teachers have never heard of CVI. CVI Scotland (cviscotland.org) provides free training resources for schools. Your child's EHC plan should name CVI explicitly and list specific environmental modifications as legal requirements.

Simple Changes Make a Big Difference

Reducing visual clutter, using high contrast materials, presenting one object at a time, and using bright colours (especially yellow and red) can dramatically improve what your child can see and engage with. A QTVI experienced in CVI can advise specifically for your child.

Your Child Can Improve

Unlike many eye conditions, CVI has the potential to improve — particularly in young children — because the developing brain is highly adaptable. Early, consistent, appropriate visual stimulation supports this. High expectations and the right environment matter enormously.
