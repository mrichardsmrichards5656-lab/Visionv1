What is Cone Dystrophy?

Cone dystrophy is a group of inherited retinal conditions in which cone photoreceptors degenerate progressively over time. Unlike achromatopsia, cone dystrophies typically begin with normal or near-normal vision that deteriorates. Affects approximately 1 in 30,000–40,000 people.

How it affects vision

Progressive loss of central vision and colour discrimination is the hallmark. Bright light sensitivity (photophobia) is prominent. Reading, recognising faces, and driving become increasingly difficult. In cone-rod dystrophy, peripheral vision and night vision are also progressively lost.

Getting a diagnosis

Electroretinography (ERG) showing preferentially reduced cone responses. OCT imaging of the macula. Colour vision testing. Genetic testing strongly recommended.

Who it affects

Affects approximately 1 in 30,000–40,000 people. Symptoms usually begin in childhood to early adulthood. Follows autosomal dominant, recessive, or X-linked inheritance depending on the gene.

Research & treatment progress

🔬 Pipeline

RPGR Gene Therapy — X-Linked Disease

Gene therapy targeting RPGR mutations (which can cause X-linked cone/cone-rod dystrophy as well as retinitis pigmentosa). MeiraGTx's Phase 3 LUMEOS trial of its RPGR therapy (bota-vec) did not meet its primary endpoint in 2025, though it showed improvements in visual acuity and patient-reported outcomes. Johnson & Johnson returned the programme to MeiraGTx in a deal completing May 2026, and MeiraGTx anticipates regulatory filings in the US, Europe and Japan during 2026. A genetic diagnosis is needed to know if you carry an RPGR mutation.

🔬 Pipeline

CNGB3 / CNGA3 Gene Therapy

Shared with achromatopsia, these mutations also cause cone dystrophy in some patients. Phase 1/2 gene therapy trials have been completed, showing acceptable safety with modest and variable improvements in vision; no trial is currently recruiting in the UK. Genetic testing determines whether you carry these mutations.

Visit website ↗

🌅 Horizon

Optogenetics for End-Stage Cone Dystrophy

For people with advanced cone dystrophy who have lost most cone function, optogenetics — delivering light-sensitive proteins to surviving cells — offers a potential route to restoring some functional vision regardless of the underlying gene mutation.

Practical guides

Get a Genetic Test

Identifying your specific gene mutation is essential for accessing trials. Request a referral to an inherited retinal disease clinic — genetic testing is free on the NHS.

Low Vision Assessment

A free NHS low vision assessment provides magnifiers, screen filters, and tinted lenses tailored to your vision. Ask your ophthalmologist for a referral.

Light Management

Use wraparound tinted lenses outdoors — amber or plum tints reduce photophobia most effectively. Use task lighting rather than ambient lighting indoors.

Helpful technology

Tinted Lenses

Photochromic & Filter Lenses

Light-reactive (photochromic) lenses and specialist absorptive filter tints (often amber or plum) markedly reduce the photophobia common in cone dystrophy and improve contrast. Best chosen with a low-vision optometrist.

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

For the central-vision reading difficulty, optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers (with contrast and colour modes) and larger desktop CCTV magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

AI Wearable

OrCam MyEye (reading & recognition camera)

Clips to any glasses frame and reads text aloud and recognises faces and products — useful when central vision makes reading tiring or difficult.

Free App

Seeing AI / Be My Eyes / Colour ID

Seeing AI reads text and identifies people, products and currency; colour-identifier apps name colours aloud (helpful given the colour-vision problems in cone dystrophy); Be My Eyes connects to a sighted volunteer.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Dark mode, high contrast, larger text and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and help with both glare and reduced acuity at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist to issue a CVI. Unlocks rehabilitation, council tax reduction, free bus pass, and free NHS eye tests.

PIP

Central vision loss and photophobia significantly affect daily tasks and travel. RNIB can help — 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

Covers tinted lenses, screen magnification software, and lighting adjustments. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work before buying privately.

Living with Cone Dystrophy

Light Management

Photophobia is often the most disabling aspect of cone dystrophy for daily life. Amber or plum wraparound tinted lenses are most effective outdoors. Indoors, reduce overhead lighting and use task lighting at source. All devices should be in dark mode at minimum brightness.

Reading and Close Work

Central vision loss makes reading, writing, and recognising faces increasingly difficult. A desktop video magnifier with adjustable contrast transforms reading. Eccentric viewing — looking slightly to the side — uses peripheral vision and can be trained with a vision rehabilitation specialist.

Driving

Progressive central vision loss from cone dystrophy will eventually affect the ability to meet the legal driving standard. Plan ahead — explore Motability, Access to Work travel support, and local transport schemes before a crisis rather than after.

Work Adjustments

Screen magnification software (ZoomText), adjusted monitor positioning, and good non-glare task lighting are all reasonable workplace adjustments. Access to Work funds specialist equipment. Apply before buying anything privately.

Genetic Testing and Trials

Your specific gene mutation determines which clinical trials you may be eligible for. Getting a genetic diagnosis is essential. RPGR trials and CNGB3 programmes are the most active — Retina UK (0300 111 4000) can advise on current recruitment.
