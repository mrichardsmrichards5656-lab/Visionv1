Charles Bonnet Syndrome

Charles Bonnet Syndrome (CBS) is a condition in which people with significant sight loss experience vivid visual hallucinations — seeing things that are not there.

What is Charles Bonnet Syndrome?

Charles Bonnet Syndrome (CBS) is a condition in which people with significant sight loss experience vivid visual hallucinations — seeing things that are not there. RNIB estimates around 1 in 5 adults with sight loss develop CBS, meaning at least 1 million people in the UK are affected. It can occur with any eye condition causing significant vision loss, including AMD, glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, and others. Despite the alarming nature of the hallucinations, CBS is not a mental health condition and does not indicate dementia or psychosis. It is caused by the brain generating its own images when deprived of normal visual input.

How it affects vision

Hallucinations vary widely — from simple patterns, grids, and flashing lights to detailed images of people, animals, buildings, and landscapes. They are always silent and usually recognised as not real by the person experiencing them. They can last seconds or hours and may occur daily or occasionally. They are often triggered by low light, tiredness, or stress. CBS can cause significant distress and anxiety, particularly if the person does not know what is happening to them.

Getting a diagnosis

CBS is diagnosed by a doctor or ophthalmologist based on the person's description of their experiences and a confirmed history of sight loss. There are no specific tests. Many people are embarrassed to mention their hallucinations — it is important to know they are very common and are a recognised medical condition.

Who it affects

Affects an estimated 1 in 5 people with significant sight loss (RNIB). More common with severe or sudden vision loss. Can affect people of any age. Significantly underreported because people fear being thought to have a mental health condition.

Research & treatment progress

🌅 Horizon

Non-Invasive Brain Stimulation (Experimental)

Researchers have tested brain stimulation techniques — transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) — aimed at calming over-active visual cortex. Evidence so far is limited to small studies and case reports with mixed results (some people improved, others did not, and one reported worsening after prolonged treatment). This is early experimental research, not an available treatment.

✅ NHS Available

Medication (Only If Hallucinations Are Intolerable)

There is no CBS-specific drug. For most people self-management is enough. When hallucinations are truly distressing, doctors may try medicines borrowed from other conditions — such as gabapentin or clonazepam (which calm over-active visual cortex), or a low dose of an antipsychotic. Evidence comes from case reports and small studies, and these carry side effects, so they are used cautiously and only when needed. Discuss with your GP or ophthalmologist.

✅ NHS Available

Self-Management Strategies Work

Eye movement techniques (rapid side-to-side movements), increasing light levels, and changing activity are proven to interrupt hallucinations in most people. Simply knowing what CBS is dramatically reduces distress — awareness is itself a treatment.

Practical guides

Tell Your Doctor

Many people suffer in silence for years because they are afraid of being thought mentally unwell. CBS is a recognised medical condition — tell your ophthalmologist or GP. Simply knowing what is happening dramatically reduces distress for most people.

Strategies to Reduce Hallucinations

Move your eyes rapidly from side to side — this interrupts the hallucination in many people. Increase light levels. Move to a different room. Some people find standing up or changing activity helps. Keep a diary of when hallucinations occur to identify your triggers.

Talk to Others

The Macular Society and RNIB both have specialist advisors who understand CBS. Talking to others with the condition — via support groups — is consistently reported as one of the most helpful things people do.

Family and Carers

Explain CBS to family members and carers. Understanding that the hallucinations are a neurological response to vision loss — not a mental health crisis — helps everyone respond calmly and supportively.

Helpful technology

Lighting

Bright, Even Lighting

Charles Bonnet hallucinations are caused by sight loss, not a mental health problem, and often strike in dim light. Raising and evening out light levels reduces episodes for many people; smart bulbs (e.g. Philips Hue) let you adjust brightness hands-free.

Self-Help

Eye-Movement & Distraction Techniques

Simple, free techniques can interrupt an episode: moving your eyes side to side (without moving your head) for a few seconds, blinking, looking away, changing the lighting, or moving to another room. The Macular Society can teach these.

Low-Vision Aids

Aids for the Underlying Sight Loss

CBS sits on top of an eye condition (often AMD), so the magnifiers, lighting and apps recommended for that condition still apply. A free NHS low-vision assessment can tailor these to you.

Support

Macular Society CBS Helpline

A dedicated Charles Bonnet helpline staffed by people who understand the condition — reassurance that the hallucinations are normal and not a sign of mental illness is itself one of the most helpful 'tools'.

Benefits & registration

PIP for Underlying Condition

CBS itself does not qualify for PIP but the underlying sight condition causing it does. Claim PIP based on your primary eye condition.

Mental Health Support

If CBS is causing significant anxiety or distress, you are entitled to mental health support via your GP. RNIB Sight Loss Counselling (0303 123 9999) also provides free sessions.

Carer Support

If CBS requires significant support from a family member or carer, they may be entitled to Carer's Allowance (£86.45/week from April 2026).
