Ocular Melanoma

What is Ocular Melanoma?

Ocular melanoma is a rare type of cancer that develops in the pigment-producing cells of the eye. The most common form, uveal melanoma, affects the choroid, ciliary body or iris. A rarer form, conjunctival melanoma, affects the clear membrane covering the white of the eye. Around 750 people are diagnosed with ocular melanoma in the UK each year. It often causes no symptoms at all and is commonly found during a routine eye test — which is why regular eye tests matter, even when vision feels normal.

How it affects vision

How much vision is affected varies considerably depending on the size and position of the tumour. Some people keep good vision throughout treatment; others experience significant sight loss in the affected eye, particularly with larger tumours or ones near the centre of the retina. Some people notice floaters, flashes of light, a growing dark spot on the iris, a shadow, blurred vision, or loss of side vision in one eye — though these symptoms can also be caused by many more common, less serious eye conditions. The aim of every treatment is to destroy the cancer cells while preserving as much vision as possible.

Getting a diagnosis

Ocular melanoma is often picked up by an optometrist during a routine eye test, before any symptoms appear. If it's suspected, you'll be referred to an ophthalmologist and then on to one of the UK's four specialist ocular oncology centres — Liverpool, London (Moorfields), Sheffield, or Glasgow — as this rare cancer is only treated at these dedicated centres. Diagnosis usually involves a detailed eye examination and an ultrasound scan; a biopsy is sometimes offered, not always to confirm the diagnosis but to help estimate the chance of the cancer spreading. A multidisciplinary team of specialists will discuss and plan the best treatment for you.

Who it affects

Ocular melanoma is rare, affecting around 750 people in the UK each year. It's more common in people with fair skin and light-coloured (blue or grey) eyes, and the average age at diagnosis is 55 to 60. Unlike skin melanoma, it isn't clearly linked to sun exposure. Certain inherited factors, including changes in the BAP1, PALB2 and MBD4 genes, can increase risk, and genetic counselling may be offered as part of your care.

📋 Good to know

Genetic Testing and BAP1

Testing for changes in the BAP1 gene, sometimes offered on a biopsy sample, can help specialists estimate the likelihood of the cancer spreading and plan appropriate monitoring. Genetic counselling is available through your specialist centre if this is relevant to you.

Visit website ↗

🌅 On the horizon

Treatments for Advanced Disease

Research continues into treatments for ocular melanoma that has spread beyond the eye, including targeted drugs and immunotherapy. Because this cancer behaves differently to skin melanoma, treatments effective for one don't always work for the other, which is an active area of study.

Practical guides

Contact Ocular Melanoma UK Early

Ocular Melanoma UK is the UK's only charity dedicated solely to this cancer. Their helpline (0300 790 0512, Monday to Friday) offers emotional and practical support to patients and family members, and they can help you understand what to expect from national treatment guidelines. Free, fully-funded psychological support sessions are available to members.

Expect to Travel for Treatment

Because this cancer is rare, it's treated at just four specialist centres in the UK: Liverpool, London, Sheffield and Glasgow. You may need to travel outside your local area for appointments and treatment — ask your care team about travel and accommodation support, including through Ocular Melanoma UK.

Ask About a Low-Vision Assessment

If treatment affects your vision, ask your GP or eye clinic for a referral to a low-vision service. They can provide magnifiers, lighting advice, and practical strategies for everyday tasks.

Wider Cancer Support

Macmillan Cancer Support and Maggie's Centres offer broader emotional, practical and financial support alongside your eye-specific care — including benefits advice, which can be especially helpful if treatment affects your ability to work.

Helpful technology

Everyday apps

Seeing AI & Be My Eyes

Free apps that read text aloud and connect you to a sighted volunteer for everyday tasks — useful if vision in the affected eye is significantly reduced. See our Useful Apps guide for more.

Magnification

Magnifiers & Screen Tools

Where vision is affected, handheld or electronic magnifiers, and the built-in magnifier and large-text settings on phones and tablets, can help with reading.

Depth perception

Adjusting to Monocular Vision

If you lose significant vision in one eye, judging distances and depth can take practice. Occupational therapy and orientation and mobility training can help you adjust, particularly for driving-related depth perception and pouring/reaching tasks.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Adults whose vision or general health is affected may be eligible for PIP. It is not means-tested and does not depend on whether you work. Special rules exist for faster processing if a condition is life-limiting — ask your specialist nurse or Macmillan for advice on whether these apply to you.

Macmillan Benefits Advice

Macmillan's benefits advisers can help you understand what financial support you may be entitled to during and after cancer treatment, including help with claims. Call free on 0808 808 00 00.

CVI & Registration

If ocular melanoma or its treatment significantly affects your sight, an ophthalmologist can complete a Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI). Registering as sight impaired can unlock further practical support.
