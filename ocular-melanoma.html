Ocular Melanoma

Ocular melanoma is a rare cancer of the eye. It often causes no symptoms and is commonly found during a routine eye test — this page explains what it is, how it's treated in the UK, and where to find support.

What is ocular melanoma?

Ocular melanoma is a rare type of cancer that develops in the pigment-producing cells of the eye. The most common form, uveal melanoma, affects the choroid, ciliary body or iris. A rarer form, conjunctival melanoma, affects the clear membrane covering the white of the eye.

Around 750 people are diagnosed with ocular melanoma in the UK each year. It often causes no symptoms at all and is commonly found during a routine eye test — which is why regular eye tests matter, even when vision feels normal.

Symptoms to be aware of

Many people have no symptoms at all before diagnosis. When symptoms do occur, they can include floaters, flashes of light, a growing dark spot on the iris, a shadow or blurred vision in one eye, or loss of side vision. These symptoms can also be caused by many more common, less serious eye conditions — but they're always worth having checked by an optician or GP.

How it's diagnosed

Ocular melanoma is often picked up by an optometrist during a routine eye test. If it's suspected, you'll be referred to an ophthalmologist and then to one of the UK's four specialist ocular oncology centres — Liverpool, London (Moorfields), Sheffield, or Glasgow — as this rare cancer is only treated at these dedicated centres.

Diagnosis usually involves a detailed eye examination and an ultrasound scan. A biopsy is sometimes offered, not always to confirm the diagnosis but to help estimate the chance of the cancer spreading. A multidisciplinary team of specialists will discuss and plan the best treatment for you.

Who it affects

Ocular melanoma is rare. It's more common in people with fair skin and light-coloured (blue or grey) eyes, and the average age at diagnosis is 55 to 60. Unlike skin melanoma, it isn't clearly linked to sun exposure. Certain inherited factors, including changes in the BAP1, PALB2 and MBD4 genes, can increase risk, and genetic counselling may be offered as part of your care.

Treatment and vision

The aim of every treatment is to destroy the cancer cells while preserving as much vision as possible. How much vision is affected varies considerably depending on the size and position of the tumour — some people keep good vision throughout treatment, while others experience significant sight loss in the affected eye.

Because this cancer is rare, treatment happens at just four specialist centres in the UK, so many people need to travel outside their local area for appointments and treatment.
