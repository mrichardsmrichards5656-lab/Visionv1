Retinoblastoma (Childhood Eye Cancer)

What is Retinoblastoma (Childhood Eye Cancer)?

Retinoblastoma is a rare type of eye cancer that can affect young children. It affects the retina, at the back of the eye. It can usually be treated successfully if it is found early, and in the UK almost all children survive it, although the affected eye often loses some or all of its sight.

How it affects vision

The main sign is a white glow or white reflection in the centre of the eye (the pupil), seen in normal or low light or in flash photos. Other signs include the eyes pointing in different directions (a squint), the coloured part of the eye changing colour, swelling around the eye, uncontrolled eye movements, vision problems, and pain in one or both eyes. A child with retinoblastoma may otherwise seem well.

Getting a diagnosis

Ask for an urgent GP or optician's appointment, or call NHS 111, if you notice any of the signs. The GP or optician will look at the back of the eyes with a light. They may refer your child straight to hospital or to an eye specialist, and a specialist appointment should happen within 2 weeks. Children are then seen at one of two UK specialist centres: the Royal London Hospital or Birmingham Children's Hospital.

Who it affects

Around 40 to 50 children are diagnosed with retinoblastoma in the UK every year, most of them under 5. About 4 in 10 cases are heritable, caused by a change in a gene called RB1 that is inherited from a parent or happens very early in development; these often affect both eyes and are usually diagnosed in the first year of life. The other 6 in 10 are not heritable, usually affect one eye, and are diagnosed at around age 2.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Treatment at a Specialist Centre

Treatment depends on the size and position of the tumour. Options are laser treatment and freezing treatment (cryotherapy) for small tumours, chemotherapy, radiotherapy including proton beam therapy, and surgery to remove the eye if the cancer has spread within it or other treatments have not worked. A removed eye is replaced with an artificial one. A child may need several rounds of treatment.

Outlook

Almost 99 to 100 in every 100 children with retinoblastoma in the UK survive for five years or more, the highest survival of any children's cancer in the UK. Treatment aims to remove the cancer, but there is a high chance of losing some or all of the vision in the affected eye.

✅ NHS Available

Screening Brothers, Sisters and Children

Close relatives of someone with retinoblastoma should be checked. Screening usually means regular eye examinations from birth to age 5, and a blood test for the RB1 gene may be possible. A relative who does not have the gene change does not need screening.

Practical guides

White Glow or New Squint: Get an Urgent Appointment

Ask for an urgent GP or optician's appointment, or call NHS 111, if you see a white glow or white reflection in your child's pupil, a swollen or painful eye, uncontrolled eye movements, or you think your child is having problems seeing. These signs should always be checked by a health professional.

Say What You Have Seen

Take the photo if a white glow showed up in a picture. Ask for the back of the eyes to be examined with a light. A specialist appointment should happen within 2 weeks of referral, so chase it if you have not heard.

Tell the Team About Family History

If a parent, brother or sister has had retinoblastoma, say so, and ask about screening for your other children and in future pregnancies.

Support Through Treatment

The Childhood Eye Cancer Trust supports families affected by retinoblastoma. The Children's Cancer and Leukaemia Group and Macmillan Cancer Support also have information for families.

Ask About Support at Nursery and School

Ask your local authority's vision impairment (sensory) service about an assessment. A Qualified Teacher of Children and Young People with Vision Impairment (QTVI) may be able to advise the nursery or school. Support is assessed and varies between areas, so ask early. Contact's free helpline for families of disabled children is 0808 808 3555.

Benefits & registration

Disability Living Allowance for Children

A child under 16 who needs more care or supervision than other children of the same age because of their sight may qualify for Disability Living Allowance. It is assessed on your child's needs, not the diagnosis, and a decision can be challenged. Apply at gov.uk/disability-living-allowance-children. In Scotland, Child Disability Payment replaces it: apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222.

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with Retinoblastoma (Childhood Eye Cancer)

Seeing With One Eye

Children who lose the sight in one eye usually adapt well. Judging distances and steps can take longer to learn. Tell nursery or school which side your child sees on so they can be seated to make the most of it.

Nursery and School

Tell the nursery or school about your child's sight early. Large print, good lighting, sitting near the front and extra time are common adjustments. Extra support is assessed by the school and local authority rather than automatic, and you can ask for an assessment.

For Parents — Retinoblastoma

Retinoblastoma is rare and frightening to hear about, but it is one of the most treatable childhood cancers when it is found early. Acting quickly on what you have noticed is the most useful thing you can do.

Trust What You Saw

The most common first sign parents notice is a white glow in the eye. If a glow or a new squint worries you, ask for an urgent appointment.

Two Specialist Centres

Children in the UK are treated at the Royal London Hospital or Birmingham Children's Hospital, working with hospitals nearer home.

Ask About Genetic Testing

About 4 in 10 cases are heritable. Genetic testing can show whether brothers, sisters or future children need eye checks.
