What is Giant Cell Arteritis?

Giant cell arteritis (GCA), also called temporal arteritis, is inflammation of the arteries, especially those at the temples. The NHS describes it as serious and in need of urgent treatment. If it is not treated quickly it can cause stroke and blindness.

How it affects vision

Symptoms can include frequent severe headaches, pain or tenderness at the temples or scalp, jaw pain when eating or talking, and vision problems such as double vision or loss of sight in one or both eyes. General symptoms include flu-like symptoms, unintentional weight loss, low mood and tiredness. If you think you might have GCA, get an urgent GP appointment or call NHS 111. Vision problems need a same-day appointment with an eye specialist.

Getting a diagnosis

A GP will ask about your symptoms, examine your temples and arrange blood tests, then refer you to a specialist. The specialist may arrange an ultrasound scan of the temples and/or a biopsy of the temporal artery under local anaesthetic.

Who it affects

Some people with GCA also develop polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR), which causes pain and stiffness in the muscles of the shoulders, neck and hips. The NHS says up to 1 in 5 people with PMR go on to develop GCA, and PMR mostly affects people over 65.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Steroid Treatment (Prednisolone)

Treatment is usually prednisolone, a steroid, started before the diagnosis is confirmed because of the risk of vision loss. A high dose is given for a few weeks, then reduced to a lower dose, which may continue for several years. A small number of people need steroids for life. Do not stop steroids suddenly unless a doctor advises it.

✅ NHS Available

Tocilizumab — If GCA Comes Back

If giant cell arteritis keeps relapsing or does not respond to other treatment, NICE (England and Wales) recommends tocilizumab, given alongside a reducing course of steroids or after steroids, for up to one year. It is only for adults who have not had tocilizumab before, and only under the agreed commercial arrangement. Ask your specialist whether it could suit you; availability in Scotland and Northern Ireland follows their own processes.

✅ NHS Available

Protecting Your Body While on Steroids

Other medicines may be added, including low-dose aspirin, stomach-protecting medicines, bisphosphonates to reduce osteoporosis risk, and immunosuppressants to help reduce the steroid dose and prevent relapse.

Practical guides

Act the Same Day

If you have a new, severe headache with scalp or temple tenderness or jaw pain when eating, especially with any change in vision, get urgent help the same day: NHS 111, an urgent GP appointment, or A&E. Treatment started quickly aims to protect your sight.

Never Stop Steroids Suddenly

Do not stop prednisolone without medical advice.

Know About Polymyalgia Rheumatica

GCA and PMR often go together. Stiffness in the shoulders, neck or hips lasting over 45 minutes after waking is a sign of PMR, so mention it to your doctor.

Bone and Stomach Protection

Long-term steroids can affect bones and the stomach. Your doctor may prescribe protective medicines; take them as advised.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides. If GCA has caused lasting sight loss, ask your consultant.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Living with Giant Cell Arteritis

Living with Long-Term Steroids

You will have regular check-ups while your steroid dose is reduced. Report new headaches, jaw pain or vision changes straight away, because symptoms can return as the dose falls.

Driving

If GCA has affected your vision, the law requires you to meet the eyesight standard and to tell the DVLA if you do not. Ask your eye specialist how your own vision measures up.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
