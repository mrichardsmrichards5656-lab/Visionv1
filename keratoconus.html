What is Keratoconus?

Keratoconus is a progressive corneal condition in which the cornea — the clear front window of the eye — gradually thins and bulges outward into a cone shape. This distorts the way light enters the eye, causing blurred and distorted vision that worsens over time. It usually affects both eyes, though one is often more severely affected. Keratoconus typically begins in the teens or early twenties and often stabilises naturally by the mid-thirties.

How it affects vision

In the early stages, vision can be corrected with glasses or soft contact lenses. As the condition progresses, glasses become increasingly ineffective and rigid contact lenses are needed to create a smooth optical surface over the irregular cornea. Scleral lenses — large rigid lenses resting on the white of the eye rather than the cornea — now allow most people to achieve excellent vision even in advanced cases. Common symptoms include blurred and distorted vision (especially at distance), frequently changing glasses prescriptions, halos and streaking around lights, and increasing light sensitivity. In a minority of advanced cases, scarring of the cornea reduces vision even with the best lenses.

Getting a diagnosis

Keratoconus is most often picked up during a routine sight test — an optometrist may notice irregular astigmatism or a pattern of frequent prescription changes. Diagnosis is confirmed using corneal topography and tomography (such as a Pentacam scan), which map the shape and thickness of the cornea precisely. Early diagnosis matters: cross-linking treatment is far more effective before significant thinning has occurred. Anyone suspected of having keratoconus should be referred to a corneal specialist promptly.

Who it affects

Keratoconus affects approximately 1 in 375 people, making it more common than many realise. It usually begins between the ages of 10 and 25, with most progression occurring in the late teens and twenties, and often stabilising by the mid-thirties. It affects males and females roughly equally, but prevalence is notably higher in South Asian populations — some studies suggest rates as high as 1 in 45 in certain groups. A family history is present in around 10–15% of cases.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Corneal Collagen Cross-Linking (CXL)

CXL is the first-line NHS treatment to halt keratoconus progression. After applying a riboflavin (vitamin B2) solution to the cornea, ultraviolet light is used to stiffen the collagen fibres and stop the cornea thinning further. It does not restore vision already lost — its job is to prevent further deterioration. A 2026 study from Birmingham and Midland Eye Centre confirmed its long-term effectiveness over two to three years. CXL is available on the NHS where there is documented evidence of progression.

🔬 Pipeline

ARGO Trial — Transepithelial (Epi-On) Cross-Linking

Standard CXL requires temporary removal of the corneal surface layer (epithelium), which causes a few days of discomfort while it heals. Transepithelial CXL (epi-on) leaves the surface intact, aiming to be less painful with faster recovery. The ARGO trial is testing a theranostic device that measures how well riboflavin has penetrated the intact epithelium during treatment. Interim results (2026, over 350 cases) show that, on average, epi-on CXL still flattens the cornea less than standard epi-off CXL — but eyes that achieved a strong theranostic reading came much closer to epi-off results, suggesting personalised guidance may be the key to making epi-on a genuine alternative. Follow-up continues.

Practical guides

Get Referred Early — It Matters

If your optometrist suspects keratoconus, ask for an urgent referral to a corneal specialist at your nearest teaching hospital. Early cross-linking is far more effective, as it halts progression before significant thinning and damage have occurred. Waiting is the one thing that makes outcomes worse.

Understanding Cross-Linking (CXL)

CXL is available on the NHS where your keratoconus is clearly progressing. It will not reverse existing changes — its purpose is to stop things getting worse. Most people take a few days off work while the corneal surface heals. Results are durable: studies following patients for ten to fifteen years find that most eyes remain stable. A minority — roughly one in ten — do continue to progress and may need repeat treatment, so you will still be monitored afterwards.

Scleral Lenses — the Vision Game-Changer

As keratoconus progresses, standard glasses stop working well. Scleral lenses are large-diameter rigid lenses that rest on the white of the eye (the sclera) rather than the irregular cornea. They create a smooth, stable optical surface and allow most people — even those with advanced keratoconus — to achieve excellent vision. Ask your ophthalmologist to refer you to a specialist scleral lens fitter.

Stop Rubbing Your Eyes

Repeated eye rubbing is strongly linked to keratoconus progression and can accelerate corneal damage. If you have allergies or persistently itchy eyes, speak to your GP about antihistamine eye drops. Treating the itch safely is far better than rubbing.

Benefits and Work Support

Keratoconus can qualify for PIP if it significantly affects daily life or the ability to travel. If vision cannot be adequately corrected even with specialist lenses, a Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI) may be appropriate — ask your ophthalmologist. Access to Work can fund specialist workplace adjustments, including screen magnification software, adjusted lighting, and specialist monitors.

Helpful technology

Specialist Lenses

Scleral Contact Lenses

Custom-fitted large-diameter rigid lenses that vault over the irregular cornea, creating a smooth optical surface. Fitted by specialist practitioners, usually via hospital eye service referral. Dramatically improve vision for most people with moderate to advanced keratoconus.

Low Vision

Tinted and Photochromic Lenses

Light sensitivity (photophobia) is common in keratoconus. Tinted or photochromic lenses that darken in bright conditions significantly reduce glare discomfort both indoors and outdoors.

Free App

Seeing AI (Microsoft)

Free AI camera app that reads text, describes scenes and identifies products through your phone camera. Useful during periods when contact lens discomfort limits wearing time.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

If keratoconus significantly affects your daily life or ability to travel safely — including the time and difficulty involved in managing specialist lenses — you may qualify for PIP. RNIB and Citizens Advice can help you complete the form.

Access to Work

If you are employed, the Access to Work grant can fund specialist workplace adjustments — including screen magnification software, adjusted monitor lighting, or taxis if contact lens discomfort affects your ability to commute.

Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI)

If your vision with best correction remains severely reduced, your consultant ophthalmologist can issue a CVI, unlocking local authority rehabilitation support and council tax reduction.

Living with Keratoconus

Managing Contact Lens Discomfort

Scleral and rigid lenses can feel unfamiliar at first, particularly at the end of a long day. Build up wearing time gradually, keep lenses scrupulously clean, and attend all aftercare appointments. If discomfort persists, go back to your fitter — small adjustments to fit or parameters often make a significant difference.

Driving and Keratoconus

Many people with keratoconus can continue driving if their vision meets the legal standard (6/12 acuity with both eyes open and adequate visual field). If your condition affects both eyes, you must notify the DVLA — this is a legal requirement. Night driving can be particularly difficult due to halos and glare; be honest with yourself about whether it is safe.

Screen Use and Work

Extended screen use can worsen light sensitivity and eye fatigue. An anti-glare filter, reduced screen brightness, and dark mode can all help. The Access to Work scheme can fund specialist workplace equipment.

The Emotional Side

A diagnosis of keratoconus — particularly in a teenager or young adult — can be frightening. The uncertainty of progression and the dependence on specialist lenses takes time to adjust to. RNIB Sight Loss Counselling (0303 123 9999) offers free, confidential support from counsellors who specialise in sight loss. The Keratoconus Group community connects you with people who genuinely understand.
