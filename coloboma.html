Coloboma

Coloboma means there is a gap or cleft in one or more of the structures of the eye, present from birth.

What is Coloboma?

Coloboma means there is a gap or cleft in one or more of the structures of the eye, present from birth. It happens when the eye does not close completely during early development in the womb. It can affect the iris (the coloured part of the eye, giving a keyhole-shaped pupil), the retina, the choroid, or the optic nerve. It can affect one eye or both. It often occurs alongside microphthalmia (a small eye), and the three conditions — microphthalmia, anophthalmia and coloboma — are together known as MACS conditions.

How it affects vision

How much vision is affected depends greatly on which part of the eye is involved and the size of the gap. Iris colobomas often affect vision very little, but can cause sensitivity to light (because more light enters the eye through the keyhole-shaped pupil). Colobomas affecting the retina or optic nerve can cause blind spots (typically in the upper part of the visual field), reduced vision, or in more severe cases significant sight loss in the affected eye. Some people with a small coloboma have completely normal vision, while others may be blind in the affected eye. Where one eye is affected and the other sees well, most people adapt and lead full, independent lives.

Getting a diagnosis

An iris coloboma may be spotted at birth from the keyhole-shaped pupil. Colobomas affecting the back of the eye are found through a detailed eye examination, including looking at the retina and optic nerve with a fundus examination. Because coloboma can be linked with wider conditions, a paediatrician or geneticist may check for associated features (for example, CHARGE syndrome). Retinal or optic-disc colobomas need ongoing monitoring, as they carry a higher risk of retinal detachment and cataract.

Who it affects

Ocular coloboma affects roughly 1 in 10,000 people. It is a genetic condition — it sometimes runs in families, but often happens by chance with no family history. Colobomas affecting both eyes are more likely to be genetic. Coloboma can occur on its own (isolated), or as part of a wider syndrome such as CHARGE syndrome. Together, the MACS conditions account for around a quarter of childhood blindness in the UK.

🌅 On the horizon

Understanding the Genes Behind Coloboma

Researchers, including teams at Moorfields Eye Hospital and UCL, are working to identify the many genes involved in MACS conditions. Better genetic understanding may in future guide diagnosis, counselling and potential treatments. Around 1 in 5 people with a MACS condition currently receive a genetic diagnosis.

📋 Good to know

Monitoring for Complications

Colobomas involving the retina or optic nerve carry a higher risk of retinal detachment and cataract over time. Regular eye checks help catch any changes early. Sudden new floaters, flashes, or a shadow across your vision should always be checked urgently.

Practical guides

Contact MACS Early

MACS (Microphthalmia, Anophthalmia & Coloboma Support) is the UK's only charity dedicated specifically to these conditions. They provide peer support, practical help, family weekends and activity trips, and the reassurance of connecting with others who understand. Their helpline is 0800 169 8088.

Manage Light Sensitivity

If an iris coloboma causes sensitivity to light, tinted or photochromic (light-reactive) lenses, a wide-brimmed hat, and good control of lighting at home can all help. An optometrist or low-vision clinic can advise on what suits you best.

Ask About a Low-Vision Assessment

If vision is affected, ask your GP or eye clinic for a referral to a low-vision service. They can provide magnifiers, lighting advice, and practical strategies. If sight loss is significant, ask about registration as sight impaired, which opens up further support.

Support at School (for children)

A child with coloboma may benefit from a Qualified Teacher of Children with Vision Impairment (QTVI). Contact your local authority's sensory support service to request an assessment, so the right support is in place in the classroom.

Helpful technology

Light sensitivity

Tinted & Photochromic Lenses

Lenses that darken in bright light can ease the glare that iris colobomas often cause, indoors and out.

Magnification

Magnifiers & Screen Tools

Where central vision is reduced, handheld or electronic magnifiers, and the built-in magnifier and large-text settings on phones and tablets, can help with reading.

Everyday apps

Seeing AI & Be My Eyes

Free apps that read text aloud and connect you to a sighted volunteer for everyday tasks — useful if vision is significantly affected. See our Useful Apps guide for more.

Benefits & registration

Disability Living Allowance (children)

Children under 16 with coloboma affecting their vision may qualify for DLA, which helps with the extra costs of a disability.

Visit website ↗

Personal Independence Payment (adults)

Adults with sight loss from coloboma may be eligible for PIP. It is not means-tested and does not depend on whether you work.

Visit website ↗

CVI & Registration

If coloboma significantly affects your sight, an ophthalmologist can complete a Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI). Registering as sight impaired can unlock further help, including a QTVI for children.
