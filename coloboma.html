Coloboma

A coloboma is a gap or cleft in one or more of the structures of the eye, present from birth. How it affects sight varies widely — from no effect at all to significant sight loss in the affected eye.

What is coloboma?

Coloboma means there is a gap or cleft in one or more parts of the eye, which is present from birth. It happens when the eye does not close completely during early development in the womb. It can affect the iris (the coloured part of the eye, often giving a distinctive keyhole-shaped pupil), the retina, the choroid, or the optic nerve. It can affect one eye or both.

Coloboma often occurs alongside microphthalmia (a small, underdeveloped eye). Together, microphthalmia, anophthalmia (absence of an eye) and coloboma are known as the MACS conditions.

How coloboma affects vision

How much vision is affected depends greatly on which part of the eye is involved and the size of the gap:

Iris coloboma

This is the most recognisable type, giving the pupil a keyhole or cat-eye shape. On its own, it often affects vision very little, but it can cause sensitivity to light, because more light enters the eye through the enlarged opening.

Retinal, choroidal or optic nerve coloboma

Colobomas affecting the back of the eye can cause blind spots — typically in the upper part of the visual field — reduced sharpness of vision, or in more severe cases significant sight loss in the affected eye. Some people with a small coloboma have completely normal vision; others may have very limited sight in the affected eye.

Where one eye is affected and the other sees well, most people adapt well and lead full, independent lives.

What causes coloboma?

Coloboma is a genetic condition. It sometimes runs in families, but often happens by chance with no family history at all. Colobomas affecting both eyes are more likely to have a genetic cause. It affects roughly 1 in 10,000 people.

Coloboma can occur on its own (an "isolated" coloboma), or as part of a wider syndrome — most notably CHARGE syndrome, which involves a specific pattern of features affecting several parts of the body. A paediatrician or geneticist can advise whether any wider assessment is helpful.

How coloboma is diagnosed

An iris coloboma may be noticed at birth from the keyhole-shaped pupil. Colobomas affecting the back of the eye are found through a detailed eye examination, including looking at the retina and optic nerve. Because coloboma can be linked with wider conditions, further checks are sometimes arranged.

Colobomas involving the retina or optic nerve need ongoing monitoring, as they carry a higher risk of retinal detachment and cataract over time.

Living with coloboma

Managing light sensitivity

If an iris coloboma causes sensitivity to light, tinted or photochromic (light-reactive) lenses, a wide-brimmed hat, and good control of lighting at home can all help. An optometrist can advise on what suits you best.

Low-vision support

If vision is affected, ask your GP or eye clinic for a referral to a low-vision service, which can provide magnifiers, lighting advice and practical strategies. If sight loss is significant, ask about registering as sight impaired, which opens up further support.

Support at school

A child with coloboma may benefit from a Qualified Teacher of Children with Vision Impairment (QTVI). Your local authority's sensory support service can arrange an assessment so the right help is in place.

Watch for these — seek urgent advice

Because coloboma of the retina or optic nerve carries a higher risk of retinal detachment, any sudden new floaters, flashes of light, or a shadow or curtain coming across your vision should be checked urgently — go to an eye casualty department or call 111. Catching a detachment early gives the best chance of protecting sight.
