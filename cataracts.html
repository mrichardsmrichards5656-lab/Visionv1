What are Cataracts?

A cataract is a clouding of the lens inside the eye, which makes sight look misty, blurred or cloudy. The lens is normally clear and helps focus light onto the back of the eye; over time, proteins in it can build up and cloud it. Cataracts mainly affect older adults, especially people aged 60 and over, and often affect both eyes, though one may be worse than the other. This page is about cataracts in adults; there is a separate page for Congenital Cataracts in children. Cataracts are treated with surgery, which is one of the most commonly performed operations on the NHS.

How it affects vision

Symptoms usually develop slowly. They can include blurred or misty vision, difficulty seeing at night, difficulty reading or watching television, sensitivity to light and glare, halos around lights, faded colours (especially blues) and double vision in one eye. You may need to change your glasses prescription more often than usual. Cataracts do not usually cause pain. If your sight changes suddenly, or you see sudden flashes, floaters or a dark curtain across your vision, that is not typical of a cataract and needs urgent attention (NHS 111 or an eye casualty).

Getting a diagnosis

A cataract is usually spotted by an optometrist (optician) during a sight test, who can refer you to a hospital eye service if needed. The eye specialist will examine your eye and talk through how the cataract affects your daily life. Treatment is not usually needed straight away. The decision to have surgery depends on how much the cataract affects your life and whether one or both eyes are involved, not on an eye-chart score alone. There may be a waiting time for surgery, and this varies across the UK.

Who it affects

You are more likely to develop cataracts if you are aged 60 or over, have had an eye injury or eye surgery, have another eye condition such as uveitis or high short-sightedness, have taken steroid medicines for a long time, smoke, have a parent or sibling who has had cataracts, or have regularly been exposed to strong sunlight without protection. Type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and severe dermatitis can also increase the risk. Women are slightly more likely to be affected.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Cataract Surgery

Surgery is the only proven treatment. The cloudy lens is broken up with ultrasound, removed, and replaced with a clear artificial lens. It is usually done under local anaesthetic (you are awake but should feel no pain), takes roughly 20 to 45 minutes, and most people go home the same day. If both eyes need surgery, they are normally done on separate days. Most people find their vision improves and recovery is quick; serious complications are rare but include infection, bleeding, inflammation, a shifted lens and retinal detachment. Standard lenses are provided on the NHS; specialist lenses, such as multifocal ones, are usually only available privately.

✅ NHS Available

Cloudy Vision After Surgery (Posterior Capsule Opacification)

Months or years after surgery, the thin membrane that holds the new lens can become cloudy, making vision blurry again. This is called posterior capsule opacification (PCO). It is not a new cataract and is treated with a quick outpatient laser procedure that makes a small opening in the membrane. Contact your optometrist or eye clinic if your vision becomes cloudy or blurry again.

✅ NHS Available

NICE Guidance on Who Should Be Offered Surgery

NICE guideline NG77 (Cataracts in adults) says decisions about surgery should be based on how the cataract affects your daily life and wellbeing, rather than on a sight-chart score alone. It covers assessment, information you should be given before surgery, and care after it. NICE guidance applies in England; the other UK nations have their own arrangements, so ask your eye clinic.

🌅 Horizon

Eye Drops to Clear Cataracts

Researchers are testing whether eye drops could one day clear or slow cataracts without surgery. Results so far come from laboratory and early-stage studies, no such drop is available or recommended, and surgery remains the only proven treatment. Be wary of any product claiming to dissolve cataracts.

Practical guides

Get Your Eyes Tested

If your sight is becoming blurred, misty or glary, book a sight test with an optometrist. They can tell whether a cataract or something else is the cause, and refer you if surgery may help. Free NHS sight tests are available to people aged 60 or over throughout the UK, and to everyone in Scotland. Sight tests are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma and some others.

Know the Warning Signs

Cataracts come on slowly. If you suddenly notice flashes of light, many new floaters, a dark shadow or curtain across your vision, or a sudden loss of sight, contact NHS 111 or an eye casualty straight away: these can signal a detached retina or another urgent problem, not a cataract.

Ask Whether Surgery Is Right for You

You do not need to wait until your sight is very poor. Talk to your optometrist or GP about how the cataract affects reading, driving, safety and daily life. Ask about waiting times, which vary, and how risks apply to you, especially if you have another eye condition such as glaucoma, diabetes-related eye disease or macular degeneration.

Before and After the Operation

Ask the clinic what to expect: stopping contact lens wear beforehand, using eye drops for about four weeks afterwards, wearing a shield at night, and avoiding rubbing the eye, swimming and heavy lifting for a while. You will need someone to take you home, and you should not drive until your vision is back to normal.

Make Your Home Safer While You Wait

Brighter lighting, stronger glasses, and high-contrast edges on steps can help while you wait. Take extra care with stairs and kerbs, and in glare from low sun or night-time headlights. Ask your local council or RNIB about home safety help if sight is affecting your daily life.

Helpful technology

Lighting

Brighter, Better-Placed Lighting

Good directional lighting often helps reading and close tasks while waiting for surgery. Place a lamp beside or behind you, pointing at the page rather than into your eyes, and reduce glare from windows and shiny surfaces.

Magnification

Handheld Magnifiers

Simple illuminated handheld or stand magnifiers can make reading labels and mail easier. An NHS low vision clinic may loan optical aids if you are referred.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma and certain other groups. Ask your optician or check NHS Business Services Authority guidance.

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Cataracts can usually be treated, so a CVI is generally only considered if your sight remains significantly reduced after treatment, or if surgery is not possible. It is free, and your consultant ophthalmologist decides. If you are not sure whether you might qualify, ask at your eye clinic. Registration can open access to local authority support and some concessions.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP, through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Living with Cataracts

Driving

You may still be able to drive if your vision meets the legal standard. You must tell the DVLA if your eyesight does not meet the minimum standard for driving, and glare from headlights or low sun can be worse with cataracts, so many people stop driving at night first. Ask your optician if you are unsure. After surgery, do not drive home yourself, and do not drive again until your vision is back to normal; your surgeon will advise.

Glare and Night Vision

Anti-glare coatings on glasses, a wide-brimmed hat, and tinted or polarised lenses may reduce glare outdoors. Plan journeys in daylight where you can, and ask your optometrist about lenses that suit you.

Reading and Everyday Tasks

Larger print, high contrast, good lighting and a magnifier can all help. Audiobooks and phone features such as screen zoom and read-aloud can reduce strain while waiting for surgery.

Work and Study

Good non-glare lighting, larger screen text and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
