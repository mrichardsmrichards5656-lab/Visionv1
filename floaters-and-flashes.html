What is Floaters and Flashes?

Floaters are small dots, lines or cobwebs that drift across your vision. Flashes are brief sparks or streaks of light you see without a real light source. Both often happen when the jelly inside the eye (the vitreous) shrinks and pulls away from the retina, which is called a posterior vitreous detachment (PVD). Most cases are harmless, but sometimes they are an early warning of a retinal tear or detachment.

How it affects vision

Floaters can look like dots, threads, cobwebs or smoke, and are most noticeable against a bright background. Flashes are usually seen in dim light. A sudden shower of new floaters, new flashes, or a dark shadow or curtain moving across your vision are signs to get help urgently.

Getting a diagnosis

An optometrist or eye doctor examines the back of your eye after drops have widened your pupil, to check for a retinal tear or detachment. This is why new symptoms should be checked even if they settle.

Who it affects

Floaters and flashes become more common with age and in people who are short-sighted. The risk of a retinal detachment is higher after eye surgery (including cataract surgery), an eye injury, or if retinal detachment runs in the family.

Check-Up and Reassurance

For most people the examination finds no tear, and no treatment is needed. Floaters that are found to be harmless may become less noticeable over time. Ask what symptoms should make you come back.

Practical guides

Know When to Act the Same Day

Get urgent advice on the same day if floaters suddenly appear or increase, you see flashes of light, a dark curtain or shadow moves across your vision, or your sight suddenly blurs. NHS advice is to call NHS 111. Where to call: NHS 111 in England, Wales and Scotland (NHS 24 in Scotland); in Northern Ireland contact your GP or out-of-hours service, or go to an emergency department.

Say When It Started

Tell the person you speak to which eye, when the symptoms began and whether you have had eye surgery, an eye injury or short sight. This helps them decide how quickly you need to be seen.

Return If Symptoms Come Back

If symptoms return or change after a check-up, seek help again promptly. NHS advice is to get medical help as soon as possible if symptoms return, because a retina can detach more than once.

Coping With Long-Term Floaters

Floaters that have been checked and found harmless may become less noticeable over time. If they are affecting daily life, tell your optometrist or GP.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Benefits & registration

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with Floaters and Flashes

Driving

Floaters that do not affect your vision usually do not stop you driving. If your sight changes, or you have had treatment, ask your doctor or optician when you can drive. The DVLA decides whether you meet the standard.

Screens and Bright Backgrounds

Floaters are most visible against white screens or bright skies. Dark mode, lower screen brightness and sunglasses outdoors can make them less distracting.
