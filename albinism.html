What is Albinism?

Albinism is a group of inherited conditions affecting melanin production, causing reduced pigmentation of the skin, hair, and eyes and — crucially — affecting the development of the visual system. Almost all forms cause visual impairment. Affects approximately 1 in 17,000 people in the UK.

How it affects vision

Visual impairment is caused by foveal hypoplasia, nystagmus, high refractive errors, photophobia, and misrouting of optic nerve fibres. Visual acuity typically ranges from 6/18 to 6/60. Stereoscopic (3D) vision is often absent or reduced.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosed by OCT (to identify foveal hypoplasia — the definitive diagnostic feature), electroretinography, and genetic testing.

Who it affects

Affects approximately 1 in 17,000 people in the UK. More prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa. Autosomal recessive in most forms.

Research & treatment progress

🌅 Horizon

Levodopa and Nitisinone Research

Researchers have explored whether drugs can improve the underdeveloped fovea in albinism. A completed randomised trial of levodopa (L-DOPA) did not show a significant overall improvement in visual acuity, though a possible trend was seen in some younger children. Separately, the US National Eye Institute is studying nitisinone to boost melanin. Neither is an established treatment — this remains early research.

🌅 Horizon

OCA1 and OCA2 Gene Therapy

Preclinical programmes developing gene replacement for OCA1 (most common form in UK) and OCA2. Early animal studies show promise. University of Manchester and international partners. Human trials likely 5–10 years away.

🌅 Horizon

Nystagmus Treatment Crossover

Treatments proven effective for nystagmus (including memantine and surgical tenotomy) can benefit people with albinism as most have associated nystagmus. Ask your ophthalmologist.

Practical guides

Sun Protection is Essential

Significantly increased risk of skin cancer due to reduced melanin. Use SPF50+ sunscreen daily, wear protective clothing and UV-blocking sunglasses. Wraparound lenses protect from UV and reduce photophobia.

Glasses and Low Vision Aids

Most people with albinism benefit from an up-to-date glasses prescription. Many also benefit from bioptic telescopic lenses. A free NHS low vision assessment is available.

Register with Your Low Vision Clinic

Low vision clinics (available via NHS referral) provide magnifiers, tinted lenses, and specialist aids free or at reduced cost.

Helpful technology

Tinted Lenses

UV-Blocking & Tinted Glasses

Wraparound UV-blocking sunglasses, tints, and photochromic (light-reactive) lenses reduce the glare and light sensitivity (photophobia) that affect almost everyone with albinism, and protect eye health outdoors. A low-vision optometrist can advise on the right tint.

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical & Electronic)

Magnification helps with reduced acuity. Optical hand and stand magnifiers may be loaned free through an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers add adjustable contrast and colour reversal.

Distance Aids

Monoculars & Bioptic Telescopes

A small handheld monocular or a bioptic telescope mounted on glasses helps with distance tasks like reading a board, signs or timetables — useful for students. Fitted through a low-vision assessment.

Screen Settings

Built-in Accessibility Settings

Enlarge text, increase contrast, and turn on zoom or magnifier features — all built into phones, tablets and computers at no cost. Often the simplest first step before buying anything.

Free App

Seeing AI / Be My Eyes

Seeing AI reads text and describes scenes through the camera; Be My Eyes connects you to a sighted volunteer for visual tasks. Both are free.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Most people with albinism qualify for sight impairment registration. Free — ask your consultant ophthalmologist.

PIP

Albinism's combined impact on vision and photophobia significantly affects daily tasks and mobility.

Free NHS Eye Tests and Optical Vouchers

Sight impairment certificate holders receive free NHS eye tests and enhanced optical vouchers.

Living with Albinism

Sun Safety Every Day

SPF50+ sunscreen on all exposed skin, every day — not just sunny days. UV damages the skin of people with albinism far more rapidly than average. Wraparound UV-blocking sunglasses protect both your eyes and the skin around them. A wide-brimmed hat adds significant protection outdoors.

Low Vision at School

Most children with albinism benefit significantly from preferential seating near the board, large print, a tablet or laptop for close-up work, and good (non-glare) lighting. A QTVI should be involved from the start of school. The Albinism Fellowship (albinism.org.uk) has specific school guidance.

Glasses and Low Vision Aids

An up-to-date glasses prescription is the foundation. Many people with albinism also benefit from bioptic telescopic lenses for distance tasks like reading the board or watching a presentation. A free NHS low vision assessment provides tailored aids — ask your ophthalmologist for a referral.

The Social Experience of Albinism

Albinism affects appearance as well as vision, and many people describe unwanted attention or questions as a significant part of their experience. The Albinism Fellowship community — including their annual conference — provides connection with others who understand both aspects of living with albinism.

Photography and Glare

Camera flash causes significant discomfort — let friends and family know. Outdoor photography in bright conditions is challenging. Tinted lenses, shade, and polarised filters on cameras can help. Many people with albinism find overcast days significantly more comfortable than bright sunshine.
