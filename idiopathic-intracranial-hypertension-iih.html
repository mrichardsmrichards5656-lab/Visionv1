What is Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (IIH)?

Intracranial hypertension is a build-up of pressure around the brain. When no cause is found it is called idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH). The raised pressure can press on the optic nerves and make them swell (papilloedema), which puts sight at risk. IIH is not usually life-threatening, but it can be a lifelong problem. Treatment can help and reduces the risk to sight.

How it affects vision

The most common symptom is headache, often worse in the morning or when coughing, straining or bending. Vision may grey out or go dark for a few seconds at a time, in one or both eyes. Some people hear a rhythmic pulsing noise in the ears, have double vision, or feel sick. Loss of vision can be permanent if raised pressure on the optic nerves is not treated, so changes in vision should be reported to a doctor straight away.

Getting a diagnosis

A GP refers suspected cases to a hospital specialist. Tests include a neurological examination, an assessment of the eyes and vision, a CT or MRI scan of the brain, and a lumbar puncture, where a needle is placed in the lower spine to measure the pressure of the fluid. IIH is diagnosed when the pressure is raised and no other cause is found. The eye clinic then monitors the optic nerves and your field of vision.

Who it affects

IIH is rare: somewhere between 1 and 3 people in every 100,000, rising to up to 20 in 100,000 among people living with obesity. About 9 in 10 people with IIH are women, mainly in their twenties and thirties, though it can affect children and adults of any age. There is a strong link with being overweight and with recent weight gain. It has also been linked to some medicines, including some antibiotics, and to high vitamin A levels.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Weight Loss

About 9 in 10 people with IIH are overweight, and losing weight is the most effective treatment: it can often reduce symptoms and can put IIH into remission. Ask your GP or specialist about weight management support.

✅ NHS Available

Medicines

Medicines that remove excess fluid or reduce the fluid around the brain, such as acetazolamide, are often prescribed, and a short course of steroids may be used to relieve headaches and lower the risk of vision loss. Tell your doctor if you are planning a pregnancy or take the contraceptive pill, because some IIH and headache medicines are not suitable in pregnancy or can make the pill less effective.

✅ NHS Available

Surgery If Sight Is at Risk

Fewer than 1 in 10 people with IIH have surgery. It is considered if other treatments fail, especially if vision is getting worse, which is treated as an emergency. Options are a shunt, a thin tube that drains excess fluid to another part of the body, and optic nerve sheath fenestration, which opens the covering around the optic nerve to relieve pressure. Surgery carries a risk of serious complications, so discuss the risks with your surgeon.

📋 Good to know

IIH:WT Trial: Weight-Loss Surgery

A UK trial funded by the NIHR and published in 2021 compared weight-loss (bariatric) surgery with a community weight management programme in 66 women with IIH and a BMI of 35 or more. After 12 months, brain pressure in the surgery group had fallen by an average of 25%, and they had lost far more weight. Weight-loss surgery is not appropriate for everyone, so ask your specialist whether it is relevant to you.

Practical guides

Know When It Is an Emergency

Call 999 or go to A&E if symptoms come on quickly, such as a sudden severe headache, a change in vision, confusion or weakness, if you have recently had a head injury, or if you have a high temperature or feel generally unwell. Tell your doctor straight away about any change in vision.

Keep Your Eye Clinic Appointments

Sight can be damaged without you noticing at first. Eye clinic checks of your optic nerves and field of vision are how the team sees whether treatment is working, even when headaches are better.

Warning Signs That It Is Getting Worse

See your doctor if headaches become more frequent or severe, if you have more episodes of vision greying out, or if the pulsing noise in your ears increases.

Pregnancy and Contraception

Tell your doctor if you are thinking about pregnancy, as some medicines used for IIH and headache can harm an unborn baby. Some headache medicines can make the contraceptive pill less effective.

Check Your Medicines

IIH has been linked to some medicines, including some antibiotics, lithium and thyroid medicines, and to high vitamin A. Do not stop anything yourself; ask your doctor to review what you take.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (IIH)

Driving

IIH can affect your field of vision and cause double vision, and headaches and some medicines can affect concentration. You must be able to meet the eyesight standard for driving, so do not drive until you have been told it is safe, and ask your doctor or optician about telling the DVLA. In Northern Ireland the DVA applies.

Headaches and Daily Life

Headaches can continue even when the eyes are stable, and there are no medicines designed specifically for IIH headache. Tell your team if headaches are affecting work or study, as headache treatment is a separate part of care.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
