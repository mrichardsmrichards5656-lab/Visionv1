What is NAION (Non-Arteritic Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy)?

Non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION) happens when the blood supply to the front of the optic nerve is reduced. The optic nerve carries signals from the eye to the brain. When it is short of blood it is damaged and swells. NAION usually affects one eye and is a common cause of sudden sight loss in people over 50. Some NHS leaflets describe it as a small stroke in the optic nerve, and it is sometimes called an "eye stroke", a term also used for retinal artery occlusion, which is a different condition.

How it affects vision

Sight loss is usually sudden and painless, in one eye. It is often noticed on waking. It can range from mild blurring or dimming to severe loss, often in the lower part of the vision, and colours can look duller. Sight can keep getting worse for some weeks before it settles. Because there is no pain, it is easy to mistake for something minor, such as sleep in the eye, so get seen the same day.

Getting a diagnosis

An eye specialist examines the optic nerve at the back of the eye, where swelling can be seen. A visual field test and an OCT scan are often arranged, and blood pressure is checked. In people over 50, blood tests are done to rule out giant cell arteritis, which can cause similar optic nerve swelling and needs urgent steroid treatment.

Who it affects

NAION is most common in people over 50. It is more likely in people born with a small, crowded optic disc, and with high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking or sleep apnoea. A sudden drop in blood pressure, such as a night-time dip during sleep, or blood loss after an accident or surgery, may play a part. In rare cases it has been linked to some medicines, including erectile dysfunction tablets and, according to the MHRA (February 2026), semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus), where it is very rare, affecting up to 1 in 10,000 people.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Same-Day Assessment and Tests

The aim is to be seen the same day at an emergency eye service or A&E. The specialist examines the optic nerve and often arranges a visual field test and an OCT scan. Blood pressure is checked, and in people over 50 blood tests are done to rule out giant cell arteritis.

✅ NHS Available

Reducing the Risk of It Happening Again

NHS leaflets say there is no proven treatment that restores sight lost to NAION. Care focuses on lowering the risk to the other eye: blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol checks, stopping smoking, and a sleep study if you snore or stop breathing in your sleep. Aspirin was used in the past, but one NHS leaflet says there is no evidence it helps and another suggests it may, so ask your eye specialist before taking it.

🌅 Horizon

Cenegermin Nasal Treatment: Phase 3 Trial

A phase 3 trial (Galassia-NAION-301) of intranasal cenegermin, a nerve growth factor treatment, against placebo was announced in July 2026. It aims to recruit about 272 people aged 50 to 80 at more than 130 centres worldwide. The company has not said which countries are included, so whether it is open in the UK is not confirmed. It is not available or recommended outside a trial.

🌅 Horizon

QPI-1007 Eye Injection: No Benefit Shown

A large phase 2/3 trial of an eye injection called QPI-1007, published in 2025, found no difference in the number of people who lost 15 or more letters of vision at 6 months compared with a sham injection. It is not available.

Practical guides

Get Seen the Same Day

Sudden loss of sight or sudden dimming in one eye needs same-day assessment even if there is no pain. Go to an emergency eye service or A&E rather than waiting for a routine optician appointment, and call NHS 111 if you are unsure where to go. Do not drive yourself. Tell staff it came on suddenly and when you noticed it.

Tell Them About Headache or Jaw Pain

If you are over 50 and also have a new headache, jaw pain when chewing, scalp tenderness, feeling unwell or aching shoulders, say so straight away. Giant cell arteritis can cause similar sight loss and needs urgent steroid treatment to protect the other eye.

Protect the Other Eye

NHS leaflets give the chance of the other eye being affected as roughly 15 to 30 in 100 over five years, and say managing risk factors may reduce it. Keep up blood pressure, diabetes and cholesterol checks, stop smoking if you can, and ask your GP about a sleep study if you snore or stop breathing in your sleep. Ask whether your blood pressure drops too low at night.

Tell Every Doctor About It

Tell any doctor, dentist or anaesthetist that you have had NAION. Before an operation under general anaesthetic, tell the anaesthetist so that very low blood pressure can be avoided. Ask your doctor about medicines linked to NAION, such as erectile dysfunction tablets, and about semaglutide if you take it. Do not stop prescribed medicines without speaking to your prescriber first.

Know What to Expect

Sight usually settles within a few weeks, and in almost all cases within two to three months. NHS leaflets say some people recover some central vision (figures quoted range from about 30 to 40 in 100), and one leaflet says central vision got worse in about 20 in 100. Missing areas of vision are unlikely to improve, though they may become less noticeable, especially if the other eye sees normally.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with NAION (Non-Arteritic Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy)

Driving

Sight loss in one eye, or a missing area of vision, can affect whether you meet the driving standard. Do not drive until you have been told it is safe, and ask your doctor or optician about telling the DVLA. In Northern Ireland the DVA applies.

Adjusting to One Good Eye

Judging distances and steps can be harder with one eye. Good lighting, contrast edging on stairs and taking time on kerbs can help. An NHS low vision service or your local council's vision rehabilitation team can offer practical training.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.

Helpline 0303 123 9999, low vision aids, talking books, and benefits advice.
