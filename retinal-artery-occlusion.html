What is Retinal Artery Occlusion?

A retinal artery occlusion (RAO) happens when the blood supply to the retina, the light-sensitive tissue at the back of the eye, is blocked. It is sometimes called an "eye stroke". It is a medical emergency and can be a warning sign of a stroke.

How it affects vision

The typical symptom is sudden, painless loss of vision in one eye. It can be complete loss, or a shadow over part of the vision. Sometimes sight goes for a few minutes and then comes back. That should also be treated as an emergency.

Getting a diagnosis

An eye specialist examines the back of the eye. Because the cause is often in the arteries or heart, other checks usually follow, such as blood pressure, blood tests, scans of the neck arteries and heart, and sometimes referral to the stroke team. In people over 50, blood tests for giant cell arteritis are also done.

Who it affects

RAO is most common in older people, especially with high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, heart disease or smoking. It is sometimes caused by giant cell arteritis, which also needs urgent treatment.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Emergency Assessment

Treatment works best when started within a few hours, so the aim is to be seen at an emergency eye service or A&E straight away. In some cases, if you arrive within hours, the stroke team may discuss clot-busting treatment, depending on local services. Many people do not regain full sight in the affected eye, though some recover part of it.

Finding and Treating the Cause

Checks on the heart, neck arteries, blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar are used to lower the risk of a stroke or of the other eye being affected. Medicines to protect against stroke may be started. Follow the advice you are given by the stroke or medical team.

Practical guides

Treat Sudden Sight Loss as an Emergency

Sudden loss of sight in one eye, even if it returns within minutes, should be treated as an emergency. Call 999 or go to A&E straight away rather than waiting for an optician appointment. Do not drive yourself. NHS advice for a suspected transient ischaemic attack (TIA) is to call 999 even if symptoms go away.

Mention Other Stroke Signs

Call 999 at once if sight loss comes with face drooping, weakness in an arm, slurred speech or confusion. Tell staff the sight loss was sudden and painless and when it began, because time matters.

Look After Your Heart and Blood Vessels

After an RAO, your team will look at blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, heart rhythm and smoking. Taking prescribed medicines and attending follow-up checks lowers the risk of a stroke or of the other eye being affected.

Ask About Giant Cell Arteritis

If you are over 50 and also have a new headache, jaw pain when chewing, scalp tenderness or aching shoulders, say so straight away. Giant cell arteritis can cause RAO and needs urgent steroid treatment to protect the other eye.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with Retinal Artery Occlusion

Driving

Sight loss in one eye can affect whether you meet the driving standard, and a stroke or TIA has its own driving rules. Do not drive until you have been told it is safe, and ask your doctor or optician about telling the DVLA. In Northern Ireland the DVA applies.

Adjusting to One Good Eye

Judging distances and steps can be harder with one eye. Good lighting, contrast edging on stairs and taking time on kerbs can help. An NHS low vision service or your local council's vision rehabilitation team can offer practical training.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.

Support organisations

Stroke Association

Information and support for people who have had a stroke or TIA, and their families. A stroke-related sight problem can also be discussed with your GP or stroke team.
