Nystagmus

What is Nystagmus?

Nystagmus is a condition causing involuntary, repetitive eye movements — most commonly side-to-side. These movements reduce visual acuity because the eyes cannot fix steadily on a target. It affects approximately 1 in 1,000 people in the UK.

How it affects vision

The involuntary movements make it difficult to focus on small or distant objects. Most people have reduced visual acuity but not complete blindness. Many find a 'null zone' — a head position where movements slow and vision is clearest.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosed by an ophthalmologist or orthoptist. In children, typically identified in the first few months of life. Investigations to identify underlying causes (MRI, ERG, genetic testing) are routinely carried out.

Who it affects

Affects approximately 1 in 1,000 people. Often associated with albinism, LCA, achromatopsia, and other inherited retinal conditions. Acquired nystagmus in adults may follow neurological events such as stroke or MS.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Tenotomy (Anderson Procedure)

A minor eye muscle surgery that shifts the null zone to a more central head position, reducing or eliminating the need for a head turn. Available at specialist centres in the UK. Ask your ophthalmologist whether you are a candidate.

Visit website ↗

🔬 Pipeline

Memantine and Gabapentin

Two existing drugs — memantine (used in dementia) and gabapentin — have been shown in randomised trials to reduce nystagmus intensity and modestly improve visual acuity, in both congenital and acquired nystagmus. They are sometimes prescribed off-label by specialists. Effects vary between individuals and side effects can occur, so discuss carefully with your ophthalmologist or neurologist.

🌅 Horizon

Treating Underlying Causes

For nystagmus caused by inherited conditions (albinism, achromatopsia, LCA), gene therapies targeting the underlying cause may secondarily reduce nystagmus severity. Early-stage research only.

Practical guides

Finding and Using Your Null Zone

The null zone is where movements are slowest and vision clearest. Ask your orthoptist to help identify yours. Employers and schools can reasonably accommodate this.

School and Exam Accommodations

Children are entitled to large print, extra time in exams, preferential seating, and reduced copying from the board. Request via your SENCo.

Driving and Nystagmus

You must inform the DVLA of your nystagmus. Many people with nystagmus cannot drive. The DVLA will assess your individual situation.

Helpful technology

Magnification

Screen Zoom & Magnification

Built-in zoom on iPhone, iPad, Android, Mac and Windows enlarges on-screen content for free and is enough for most people. ZoomText (now sold as a subscription via Freedom Scientific/VisionAid — price varies) adds advanced magnification with speech for heavier needs.

Reading Aid

Large Print, Line Guides & Overlays

Large-print books are free through the RNIB Library. A typoscope or line guide helps keep your place, and coloured overlays can ease reading for some people. Cheap and widely available.

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical & Electronic)

Optical hand and stand magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers add adjustable size, contrast and colour. Useful where head-posture (null point) makes small print tiring.

Distance Aids

Monocular Telescope

A small handheld monocular helps with distance tasks such as reading a whiteboard, signs or bus numbers — handy for school, college and travel. Best fitted via a low-vision assessment.

Free App

Seeing AI / Be My Eyes

Read text aloud and identify objects with Seeing AI, or get live help from a sighted volunteer with Be My Eyes, when small print or distance is difficult.

Benefits & registration

PIP for Nystagmus

If nystagmus significantly reduces your ability to read, navigate, or work, you may be entitled to PIP. RNIB provides free help — 0303 123 9999.

Free Eye Tests

CVI holders are entitled to free NHS eye tests. Register with your optician.

Workplace Adjustments

Under the Equality Act 2010, employers must provide reasonable adjustments including screen magnification and adjusted seating.

Living with Nystagmus

Finding and Using Your Null Zone

Your null zone — the head or eye position where movements slow and vision is clearest — is your most important tool. An orthoptist can help identify yours precisely. At a desk, adjust monitor height and position so your null zone aligns with your screen naturally. Employers and schools must accommodate this as a reasonable adjustment.

School and University

Extra time in exams, large print, reduced copying from the board, and preferential seating are all standard adjustments. Request them proactively via your SENCo at school or disability services at university. The Nystagmus Network (nystagmusnetwork.org) has specific guidance for students.

Driving

Many people with nystagmus cannot meet the visual standard for driving. Inform the DVLA honestly. If you cannot drive, explore Motability alternatives — wheelchair taxis, community transport, and local authority travel schemes — as early as possible.

Screen and Reading Strategies

Increase font size significantly and use high contrast mode on all devices. Screen magnification software (ZoomText) helps for computer work. Reading stands and book holders allow you to position text in your null zone without holding it.

Explaining to Others

Nystagmus is often invisible to others — people may not understand why vision is affected. A brief, clear explanation helps in social and work situations. The Nystagmus Network has printable information cards worth carrying.

For Parents — Nystagmus

Nystagmus is usually identified in the first few months of life. Here is what you need to know to support your child.

Your Child Will Adapt

Children born with nystagmus adapt to their vision from the start. They learn to use their null zone naturally, develop strategies for focusing, and in most cases attend mainstream school and live fully independent lives.

Finding the Null Zone

Your child's null zone — the head or eye position where the movements slow and vision is clearest — is important for school. An orthoptist can identify it precisely. Ensure teachers understand it and arrange seating accordingly.

School Adjustments

Your child is entitled to preferential seating, large print, extra time in exams, and reduced copying from the board. The Nystagmus Network (nystagmusnetwork.org) has specific guidance packs for schools and parents.

DVLA and Driving

Your child will need to inform the DVLA about their nystagmus when they reach driving age. Many people with nystagmus cannot drive — prepare your child for this and explore independence alternatives early.

Nystagmus Network

The UK's dedicated nystagmus charity provides helpline support, school guidance packs, and a community of families. Connecting with other families is consistently the most helpful thing parents describe — nystagmusnet.org.
