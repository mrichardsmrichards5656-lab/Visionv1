Microphthalmia

Microphthalmia is a congenital condition (present from birth) in which one or both eyes develop smaller than normal during pregnancy.

What is Microphthalmia?

Microphthalmia is a congenital condition (present from birth) in which one or both eyes develop smaller than normal during pregnancy. It ranges from a slightly small eye with good vision to a very small eye with little or no sight. It affects around 8.6 per 100,000 infants in the UK (roughly 53 newly diagnosed babies per year). It often occurs alongside anophthalmia (absence of an eye) or coloboma (a cleft or gap in the eye) — together known as MACS conditions. Around 114 babies are born with a MACS condition in the UK each year.

How it affects vision

The impact on vision depends entirely on severity. Some children with mild microphthalmia have useful vision, while others are blind in the affected eye. When only one eye is affected, the other eye can often develop good vision. Many children wear a prosthesis (an acrylic artificial eye) over the affected eye to help the face grow normally and for cosmetic reasons — this is remoulded every few months as the child grows. Microphthalmia is frequently associated with other conditions, and some children have additional learning, developmental, or health needs.

Getting a diagnosis

Microphthalmia is usually diagnosed at birth or during newborn screening. A baby with suspected microphthalmia should be seen at an eye clinic as soon as possible. Tests may include: an ultrasound scan to measure the size of the eye, electrodiagnostic tests to check whether the eye can see, MRI or CT scans to look at the structure of the eye and brain, and a genetic blood test to see whether a change in genes caused the condition. Diagnosis often involves a multidisciplinary team because of associated conditions.

Who it affects

Microphthalmia is present from birth and affects boys and girls equally. Genetic causes include changes in genes such as SOX2, OTX2, RAX, PAX6 and CHX10. Potential environmental factors during pregnancy include gestational infections (such as rubella and toxoplasmosis), vitamin A deficiency, and exposure to alcohol or certain drugs. It often co-occurs with other developmental conditions. There is currently no treatment to restore vision — management focuses on preserving existing sight and supporting development.

Research & treatment progress

🔬 Pipeline

Genetic Diagnosis Research (Moorfields)

Research led by Professor Mariya Moosajee at Moorfields and the NIHR Biomedical Research Centre has increased the rate of identifying genetic causes of microphthalmia from 8% to 33%, using data from the UK's 100,000 Genomes Project. Better genetic diagnosis improves counselling and is the foundation for future treatments.

Visit website ↗

🌅 Horizon

3D Eye Organoid Models

Scientists are growing 3D models of the developing human eye using stem cell technology to understand how microphthalmia develops and to test potential treatments. This research also identified that the childhood cataract gene EPHA2 is involved in microphthalmia. Still at preclinical (laboratory) stage — no human trials yet.

Practical guides

Contact MACS Early

MACS (Microphthalmia, Anophthalmia & Coloboma Support) is the UK's only charity dedicated specifically to these conditions. They provide peer support, practical help, and family activity trips. Call 0800 169 8088 — connecting with other families early makes a real difference.

See a Specialist Quickly

A baby with microphthalmia should be seen by an ophthalmologist as soon as possible, ideally at a specialist centre such as Moorfields. Early assessment guides prosthesis fitting and protects any vision in the better eye.

Protect the Better Eye

If one eye is affected, protecting the sight in the other eye is vital. Ask about safety glasses for sport and play, and ensure regular monitoring of the unaffected eye.

Prosthesis and Facial Growth

A prosthesis (artificial eye or conformer) is often fitted to help the eye socket and face develop normally. It needs remoulding every few months as your child grows. This is managed by an ocularist working with the eye clinic.

Genetic Counselling

Ask for a referral for genetic testing and counselling. This helps identify the cause, understand the chance of it happening in future pregnancies, and may open doors to research studies.

Educational Support

Request a QTVI (Qualified Teacher of Visual Impairment) from your local authority. Children with microphthalmia may need support in school whether they have sight in one eye or significant vision loss.

Helpful technology

Prosthetics

Artificial Eye / Conformer

A custom acrylic prosthesis or conformer, made and fitted by an ocularist on the NHS, helps the eye socket and face develop normally and is remoulded periodically as the child grows.

Protection

Protective Eyewear for the Better Eye

When one eye is affected, protecting sight in the other eye is vital. Polycarbonate safety glasses for sport and play guard against injury. Ask the eye clinic or optometrist.

Vision

Low-Vision Aids for Residual Sight

For children with some remaining vision, magnifiers, good lighting and high-contrast, low-clutter materials help. A local low-vision clinic and a QTVI (qualified teacher of vision impairment) assess and tailor these.

Access

Screen Readers, Braille & Tactile Learning

Where vision is very limited, built-in VoiceOver (Apple) and TalkBack (Android) are free, and early tactile and braille learning (supported by a QTVI) builds literacy. Braille displays are fundable through education support.

Support

MACS Family Support

MACS is the UK charity for microphthalmia, anophthalmia and coloboma — peer support, family weekends and activity trips for children and families.

Benefits & registration

Disability Living Allowance (DLA)

Children under 16 with microphthalmia are likely eligible for DLA. Call RNIB (0303 123 9999) for free help with the form. Apply early — the process takes time and backdating is not available.

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

If your child meets the criteria, ask the ophthalmologist about registering them as sight impaired or severely sight impaired. This unlocks practical support and benefits.

Carer's Allowance

If you provide significant care, you may be entitled to Carer's Allowance (£86.45/week from April 2026). Your child's DLA may also qualify the household for additional support.

Special Educational Needs Support

Your child may be entitled to an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP). Contact your local authority's SEND team and request a QTVI assessment.
