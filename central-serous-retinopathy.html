What is Central Serous Retinopathy?

Central serous retinopathy (CSCR, also written CSC) happens when fluid collects under the retina, the light-sensitive tissue at the back of the eye. It occurs when the cells that pump fluid out do not work properly, and/or when the layer of blood vessels underneath is abnormal. It usually affects one eye.

How it affects vision

Symptoms include painless blurring of central vision, objects looking distorted or smaller, straight lines looking curved, and difficulty reading small print. They usually last a few months but can last longer, and in some people leave lasting vision loss.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosis is made after an eye examination and an OCT scan, and sometimes a dye test (angiography) to locate the leak.

Who it affects

CSCR mainly affects adults aged 30 to 50 and is more common in men. Steroids are a known trigger, including tablets, inhalers, nasal sprays, creams and drops. Major stress is also linked to it. Never stop a steroid on your own: talk to the person who prescribed it.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Observation (Watchful Waiting)

In most people (Moorfields says about 85%) the fluid clears by itself within one to six months, so the first approach is usually regular monitoring and reviewing any steroid use with your prescriber.

🌅 Horizon

Photodynamic Therapy and Laser

If fluid lasts beyond four to six months, or keeps coming back and slowly reduces vision, a 'cold laser' with a light-sensitive dye (photodynamic therapy) or other laser may be considered. Moorfields notes that no treatment is licensed or NICE-approved for CSCR, so funding may need to be requested and can cause delays.

🌅 Horizon

Eplerenone and Spironolactone Tablets

Some people improve on these tablets, but evidence is mixed and blood potassium levels need monitoring. Ask your eye specialist whether they are suitable.

Practical guides

Tell Your Team About Any Steroids

Steroids in any form can trigger CSCR. Make sure your eye doctor knows about tablets, inhalers, nasal sprays, creams, eye drops and injections. Do not stop a steroid yourself; your prescriber can review whether it is still needed.

Check Each Eye in Turn

Covering each eye in turn and looking at straight lines, such as a door frame, helps you notice distortion. Tell your clinic if the blur or distortion gets worse or does not settle.

Wait Before Changing Glasses

Moorfields advises that new glasses do not treat CSCR, because the prescription shifts as the fluid changes. It is best to wait until the fluid has fully cleared.

Look After Stress and Sleep

Major stress is linked to CSCR in some people. Rest, regular sleep, talking to someone and, if needed, asking your GP about support may help, alongside your eye care.

Know When to Seek Help Quickly

A sudden new loss of vision, a shadow or curtain, or new flashes and floaters needs same-day advice. See our Eye Emergencies page. Where to call: NHS 111 in England, Wales and Scotland (NHS 24 in Scotland); in Northern Ireland contact your GP or out-of-hours service, or go to an emergency department.

Ask for a Low Vision Assessment

If your sight stays reduced, ask your ophthalmologist or optometrist for a referral to an NHS low vision service — the assessment is free. They work from what you actually struggle with (reading, screens, faces, getting about) and match aids and lighting to it. Optical magnifiers are normally loaned free for as long as they help; electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not funded.

Helpful technology

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Magnification

Magnifiers (Optical, Handheld & Desktop)

Optical magnifiers may be loaned free via an NHS low-vision clinic; electronic handheld magnifiers and larger desktop video magnifiers offer more power for longer reading.

Free App

Seeing AI and Be My Eyes

Two free smartphone apps. Seeing AI uses the phone camera to read text aloud and describe products and scenes; Be My Eyes connects you with a sighted volunteer by video for help with a task.

Benefits & registration

Certificate of Vision Impairment (CVI)

Ask your consultant ophthalmologist about a Certificate of Vision Impairment, which is free. Registration can open access to local authority rehabilitation and support, free NHS sight tests and travel concessions. A council tax reduction is sometimes possible but is not automatic, so ask your council. Whether you qualify depends on how much sight you have, and your consultant decides.

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

Awards depend on how your sight affects daily life, not on the diagnosis. Apply at gov.uk/pip or call 0800 917 2222. In Scotland, Adult Disability Payment replaces PIP — apply through Social Security Scotland on 0800 182 2222. RNIB can help with the form on 0303 123 9999.

Access to Work

A government grant towards workplace adjustments and specialist equipment. Apply at gov.uk/access-to-work. There is currently a long waiting list, so apply as early as you can.

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Living with Central Serous Retinopathy

Driving

Many people can keep driving if the other eye is unaffected, but central blur and distortion can affect your ability to meet the standard. Ask your doctor or optician about telling the DVLA.

Reading and Screens

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode, good lighting and a magnifier can help while the fluid clears. Take breaks, as straining to read through distortion is tiring.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
