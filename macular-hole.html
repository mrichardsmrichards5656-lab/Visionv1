What is Macular Hole?

A macular hole is a small, full-thickness break in the macula — the central area of the retina responsible for detailed, sharp, central vision. It most commonly develops in people over 60 as a result of age-related changes to the vitreous gel inside the eye: as the vitreous slowly shrinks and pulls away from the retina, it can create a tear at the macula itself. Macular holes are graded by size and severity in four stages. They are more common in women and are bilateral (affecting both eyes eventually) in up to 10% of cases. The good news is that surgery is highly effective — UK studies report a closure rate of around 96%.

How it affects vision

A macular hole causes a blurred, distorted, or missing patch at the very centre of vision. You may notice: a blurry or absent spot in the middle of what you are looking at; straight lines appearing wavy or bent (a symptom called metamorphopsia); difficulty reading or recognising faces; and colours that appear washed out centrally. Peripheral vision is preserved. Symptoms typically develop gradually, and many people first notice the problem when covering their good eye by accident.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed with an OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography) scan, which produces a detailed cross-section of the retina and can visualise the hole precisely, grade it by size, and guide the surgical approach. If your optometrist notices anything on a routine eye test, they should refer you urgently to a retinal specialist. The OCT also helps surgeons plan the operation and assess recovery after surgery. If you notice a new blurred or distorted patch in the centre of your vision, see your optometrist the same day — earlier surgery gives better outcomes.

Who it affects

Macular holes affect approximately 1 in 200 people over the age of 60. Women account for around 71% of surgical cases (based on a UK study of more than 1,400 operations). They can also develop in younger people following eye trauma or as a complication of severe short-sightedness (myopia). Up to 10% of people who develop a macular hole will eventually develop one in the other eye, so the second eye is monitored carefully after diagnosis.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Vitrectomy Surgery — 96% Closure Rate in UK Studies

Vitrectomy is the standard NHS treatment for macular holes. The surgeon removes the vitreous gel and carefully peels away a thin membrane (the internal limiting membrane, ILM) surrounding the hole, then fills the eye with a gas bubble that holds the edges of the hole together as it heals. A large UK study of 1,253 operations published in the journal Eye found successful closure in 96% of cases. Vision typically improves significantly after closure, though some central distortion may remain. Smaller holes carry the highest success rates.

🔬 Evolving Guidance

Face-Down Positioning After Surgery — Changing Practice

Patients were historically required to lie face-down for up to two weeks after vitrectomy to keep the gas bubble in contact with the hole. The large UK cohort study above found that post-operative positioning did not significantly affect whether the hole closed, and the UK PIMS randomised trial found face-down positioning did not improve closure rates for large holes, though it suggested a modest visual acuity benefit at three months. A 2024 international review pooling individual patient data concluded the evidence is still not strong enough to say definitively whether positioning matters differently for different hole sizes. Practice varies between surgeons — your surgeon will advise based on your specific hole.

Practical guides

Act Quickly — Earlier Surgery Gives Better Results

Macular holes tend to enlarge over time if untreated, and larger holes have lower closure rates and poorer visual outcomes. Once referred, don't delay. Most UK vitreoretinal surgeons aim to operate within one to three months of referral — within this window is good, but try not to let it extend much beyond that.

What to Expect From Surgery

Vitrectomy is usually performed under local anaesthetic as a day procedure. It takes around an hour. Afterwards, your eye will be red and sore for a few days, and you'll have eye drops to use for several weeks. Vision will be blurry while the gas bubble is present. The bubble absorbs gradually over six to eight weeks, and vision improves during that time.

You Must Not Fly With a Gas Bubble

After vitrectomy, you must not travel by air or go to high altitude until the gas bubble has completely disappeared. The drop in air pressure causes gas to expand rapidly, which can cause serious damage to the eye. Tell your surgeon about the gas bubble before any planned operation under general anaesthetic for other conditions — even if unrelated to the eye.

Watch the Other Eye

After a macular hole in one eye, the other eye carries a higher risk. Test it regularly with an Amsler grid: look at the central dot with one eye covered, one eye at a time. If any new distortion, waviness, or missing area appears, contact your eye clinic the same day. Catching a hole early gives the best outcomes.

Benefits if Vision Does Not Fully Recover

If macular hole surgery doesn't fully restore your central vision, you may be left with lasting visual impairment. PIP can be claimed if daily life is significantly affected. If vision in the affected eye is severely reduced, a CVI may be appropriate — ask your ophthalmologist. A referral to a low vision clinic is free and can make a real difference to daily function.

Helpful technology

Monitoring

Amsler Grid

A simple grid used to monitor your central vision at home. Cover one eye and look at the centre dot — wavy, missing, or distorted lines need same-day attention. Particularly important for monitoring the second eye after a macular hole diagnosis.

Smart Magnifier

Desktop Video Magnifier (CCTV)

Magnifies text and objects on a large screen with adjustable contrast and colour modes. Very useful if central vision remains reduced after surgery. Ask your ophthalmologist for a low vision referral: the assessment is free and optical magnifiers are normally loaned free, but electronic and desktop video magnifiers are usually not NHS-funded. Most clinics will let you try one before you buy, and a few services do loan them — worth asking.

Free App

Seeing AI (Microsoft)

Free app that reads printed text, handwriting, and documents aloud through your phone camera. Helpful when central vision is temporarily or permanently reduced.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

If macular hole surgery leaves lasting central vision loss that affects daily activities or your ability to travel safely, you may be eligible for PIP. The functional impact is what matters. RNIB Benefits Helpline (0303 123 9999) can advise.

Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI)

If vision in the affected eye is severely reduced after surgery, your consultant ophthalmologist can issue a CVI. If both eyes are significantly affected, a CVI is more likely. It unlocks local authority rehabilitation support and, for those registered as severely sight impaired, a 50% reduction on the TV licence.

NHS Low Vision Clinic

After surgery, ask your ophthalmologist for a referral to a low vision clinic. These free NHS services assess your remaining vision and prescribe magnifiers, lighting, and other aids to help you manage daily life.

Living with Macular Hole

Recovery Expectations

Vision after macular hole surgery typically improves gradually over three to six months as the retina heals. Most people see significant improvement, but some central distortion or blur may remain permanently. The first few weeks are the hardest: the gas bubble severely limits what you can see, making reading and faces very difficult. This is entirely normal and temporary.

Reading and Daily Life During Recovery

While the gas bubble is present, reading print is very difficult. RNIB Talking Books (free), BBC Sounds, and audiobooks on a phone keep reading accessible without any need to see a screen. Large-button phones and voice assistants (Alexa, Google Home, Siri) can manage calls, timers, and reminders hands-free.

The Emotional Impact

Even with successful surgery, the uncertainty and temporary sight loss are stressful. The Macular Society's befriending service connects you with trained volunteers who have lived experience of macular conditions. RNIB Sight Loss Counselling (0303 123 9999) offers free, confidential counselling from specialists who understand sight loss — available before, during, and after treatment.

Protecting the Other Eye

After a macular hole in one eye, regular Amsler grid monitoring of the other eye is important. A daily test takes under a minute. If any new distortion appears, contact your eye clinic the same day. A hole caught early almost always closes successfully with surgery.
