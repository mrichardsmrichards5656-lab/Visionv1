Macular Hole

What is Macular Hole?

A macular hole is a small, full-thickness break in the macula — the central area of the retina responsible for detailed, sharp, central vision. It most commonly develops in people over 60 as a result of age-related changes to the vitreous gel inside the eye: as the vitreous slowly shrinks and pulls away from the retina, it can create a tear at the macula itself. Macular holes are graded by size and severity in four stages. They are more common in women and are bilateral (affecting both eyes eventually) in up to 10% of cases. The good news is that surgery is highly effective — UK studies report a closure rate of around 96%.

How it affects vision

A macular hole causes a blurred, distorted, or missing patch at the very centre of vision. You may notice a blurry or absent spot in the middle of what you are looking at; straight lines appearing wavy or bent (a symptom called metamorphopsia); difficulty reading or recognising faces; and colours that appear washed out centrally. Peripheral vision is preserved. Symptoms typically develop gradually, and many people first notice the problem when covering their good eye by accident.

Getting a diagnosis

Diagnosis is confirmed with an OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography) scan, which produces a detailed cross-section of the retina and can visualise the hole precisely, grade it by size, and guide the surgical approach. If your optometrist notices anything on a routine eye test, they should refer you urgently to a retinal specialist. If you notice a new blurred or distorted patch in the centre of your vision, see your optometrist the same day — earlier surgery gives better outcomes.

Who it affects

Macular holes affect approximately 1 in 200 people over the age of 60. Women account for around 71% of surgical cases, based on a UK study of more than 1,400 operations. They can also develop in younger people following eye trauma or as a complication of severe short-sightedness (myopia). Up to 10% of people who develop a macular hole will eventually develop one in the other eye, so the second eye is monitored carefully after diagnosis.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Vitrectomy Surgery — 96% Closure Rate in UK Studies

Vitrectomy is the standard NHS treatment. The surgeon removes the vitreous gel and carefully peels away a thin membrane (the internal limiting membrane, ILM) surrounding the hole, then fills the eye with a gas bubble that holds the edges together as it heals. A large UK study of 1,253 operations published in the journal Eye found successful closure in 96% of cases. Vision typically improves significantly after closure, though some central distortion may remain. Smaller holes carry the highest success rates.

🔬 Evolving Guidance

Face-Down Positioning After Surgery — Changing Practice

Patients were historically required to lie face-down for up to two weeks after surgery. A large UK study found that post-operative positioning made no statistically significant difference to outcomes for many hole sizes. A 2026 national survey of UK vitreoretinal surgeons confirmed that most now use minimal or no face-down positioning for smaller holes. Your surgeon will advise based on the size of your specific hole.

Practical guides

Act Quickly — Earlier Surgery Gives Better Results

Macular holes tend to enlarge over time if untreated, and larger holes have lower closure rates and poorer visual outcomes. Once referred, don't delay. Most UK vitreoretinal surgeons aim to operate within one to three months of referral.

What to Expect From Surgery

Vitrectomy is usually performed under local anaesthetic as a day procedure, taking around an hour. Afterwards your eye will be red and sore for a few days, and you'll have eye drops for several weeks. Vision will be blurry while the gas bubble is present. The bubble absorbs gradually over six to eight weeks, and vision improves during that time.

You Must Not Fly With a Gas Bubble

After vitrectomy you must not travel by air or go to high altitude until the gas bubble has completely disappeared. The drop in air pressure causes gas to expand rapidly, which can seriously damage the eye. Tell any anaesthetist about the gas bubble before any operation under general anaesthetic, even if unrelated to the eye.

Watch the Other Eye

After a macular hole in one eye, the other eye carries a higher risk. Test it regularly with an Amsler grid: cover one eye and look at the central dot, one eye at a time. If any new distortion, waviness, or missing area appears, contact your eye clinic the same day. Catching a hole early gives the best outcomes.

Helpful technology

Monitoring

Amsler Grid

A simple grid used to monitor central vision at home. Cover one eye and look at the centre dot — wavy, missing, or distorted lines need same-day attention. Particularly important for monitoring the second eye after a macular hole diagnosis.

Smart Magnifier

Desktop Video Magnifier (CCTV)

Magnifies text and objects on a large screen with adjustable contrast and colour modes. Very useful if central vision remains reduced after surgery. Available free via NHS low vision clinic — ask your ophthalmologist for a referral.

Free App

Seeing AI (Microsoft)

Free app that reads printed text, handwriting, and documents aloud through your phone camera. Helpful when central vision is temporarily or permanently reduced.

Benefits & registration

Personal Independence Payment (PIP)

If macular hole surgery leaves lasting central vision loss that affects daily activities or your ability to travel safely, you may be eligible for PIP. The functional impact is what matters. RNIB Benefits Helpline (0303 123 9999) can advise.

Certificate of Visual Impairment (CVI)

If vision in the affected eye is severely reduced after surgery, your consultant ophthalmologist can issue a CVI. If both eyes are significantly affected, a CVI is more likely. It unlocks local authority rehabilitation support and, for those registered severely sight impaired, a free TV licence.

NHS Low Vision Clinic

After surgery, ask your ophthalmologist for a referral to a low vision clinic. These free NHS services assess your remaining vision and prescribe magnifiers, lighting, and other aids to help you manage daily life.
