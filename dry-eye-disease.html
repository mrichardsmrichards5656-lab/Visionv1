What is Dry Eye Disease?

Dry eyes are common and usually not serious. They happen when you don't make enough tears, or your tears evaporate too quickly. This leaves the surface of the eye uncomfortable and can sometimes affect your sight. This page is about dry eye disease; blepharitis (inflamed eyelids), which often goes with it, has its own page.

How it affects vision

Symptoms can include itchy, sore, gritty or red eyes, blurry vision, sensitivity to light, and eyes that are more watery than normal. Get emergency help (999 or A&E) if your vision changes, such as wavy lines, flashing lights or loss of sight, if looking at light hurts, or if you have a red eye with severe headache and nausea. A painful red eye, or a red eye while wearing contact lenses, needs urgent advice from a GP or NHS 111, as it could be an infection.

Getting a diagnosis

An optometrist (optician) or GP can look for the cause and recommend treatment. If no cause is found, they may refer you to an eye specialist (ophthalmologist) for tests. See them if dry eyes last more than a few weeks despite home treatment, or if the shape of your eyelids changes.

Who it affects

You are more likely to have dry eyes if you are over 50, wear contact lenses, spend long periods looking at screens, work or live in air-conditioned, heated, dry, dusty or smoky places, smoke or drink a lot of alcohol, or take certain medicines, including some antidepressants and blood pressure medicines. Conditions such as blepharitis, Sjögren's syndrome and lupus are also linked.

Research & treatment progress

✅ NHS Available

Self-Care First

The NHS recommends cleaning your eyelids daily (a warm, not hot, flannel, gentle massage of the lids, then wiping away oil and crusts with warm cotton wool), taking regular screen breaks with the screen just below eye level, using a humidifier, resting your eyes from contact lenses, and avoiding smoke and dry or dusty rooms.

Treating the Underlying Cause

Your optician or GP can look for a cause, such as a medicine, blepharitis or an autoimmune condition, and treat or manage it. Do not stop a prescribed medicine without medical advice, even if you think it is causing dry eyes.

Practical guides

Keep Your Eyelids Clean

Daily lid care is the NHS's main self-care advice: a warm flannel on closed lids, a gentle massage, then wipe away oil, crusts and debris with warm cotton wool. See our Blepharitis page if your lids are sore or crusty.

Take Screen Breaks

We blink less when we look at screens, so tears evaporate faster. Take regular breaks and position the screen just below eye level.

Look After Contact Lenses

Rest your eyes by wearing glasses instead of contact lenses for a while. A red eye while wearing contact lenses needs urgent advice from a GP or NHS 111.

Know When It Is Urgent

Get urgent help for a painful red eye. Go to A&E or call 999 if your vision changes, light hurts your eyes, or you have a very dark red eye or a red eye with severe headache and nausea. Do not drive yourself.

See Your Optician If It Persists

If dry eyes last more than a few weeks despite home treatment, or the shape of your eyelids changes, see an optician or GP to find the cause.

Helpful technology

Screen Settings

Dark Mode, Contrast & Zoom

Larger text, high contrast, dark mode and screen zoom are built into all phones, tablets and computers and can reduce glare and strain at no cost.

Environment

Humidifier and Screen Position

The NHS suggests using a humidifier to add moisture to the air and positioning your screen just below eye level.

Benefits & registration

Free NHS Sight Tests

Sight tests are free on the NHS if you are aged 60 or over (throughout the UK), and for everyone in Scotland. They are also free for people with diabetes or glaucoma, people registered as sight impaired, and some others, including children under 16. Ask your optician.

Reasonable Adjustments at Work

If dry eyes make screen work uncomfortable, ask your employer about adjustments such as screen breaks, positioning and lighting. Employers must consider reasonable adjustments. Access to Work may help if the effect is significant; apply at gov.uk/access-to-work.

Living with Dry Eye Disease

Screens and Environment

Take regular breaks, avoid smoky, dusty and dry rooms, and think about heating and air conditioning, which dry the air.

Driving

Dry eyes do not normally stop you driving, but blurred vision can affect your sight. Blink and rest your eyes, and stop driving if your vision is not clear. Ask your optician if you are unsure.

Work and Study

Screen magnification, good non-glare lighting and adjusted monitor positioning are reasonable adjustments that employers must consider. Access to Work can help fund specialist equipment, though waits are long, so apply early.
